== intro
## Welcome

This course takes you from your first words of Spanish to the point where you can follow a conversation, read a newspaper and say what you think. There are a hundred lessons, one a day: in a little over three months you will have come a long way, and without strain, if you keep at it.

The Spanish of these lessons is the Spanish of Spain, as it is spoken in Madrid: its words, its spelling and its pronunciation, with the c and z said like the th of think, vosotros for "you all", coche, ordenador, móvil and zumo. With this Spanish you will be understood all over the Spanish-speaking world, where some words and sounds are different: the notes say so when it matters, and lessons 92 to 94 tell you about the main differences. Most scenes take place in Spain, in bars, flats, shops, stations and offices, in Madrid above all, but also in Toledo, Valencia, Pamplona, Asturias, Andalusia and on the Way of St James, because a language is best learnt inside the life of the people who speak it.

## A few tips

- *Half an hour a day.* Regularity matters more than anything. One lesson a day is enough: a little every day is better than a lot once a week. If one day you only have five minutes, listen again to the previous day's dialogue and repeat it aloud: the home page offers it to you.
- *The first lessons are the strangest.* The rolled r, the rough j, the th of gracias, sentences you do not understand yet: that is normal. After a week your ear begins to get used to them.
- *Do not learn anything by heart.* Neither rules nor lists of words. Read, listen and repeat: the words and the patterns come back again and again until they are yours. You will often meet a form before a note explains it: that is fine, it is the way a child learns its own language.
- *Speak aloud.* Repeat every sentence, copying the voice, the rhythm and the tune of the sentence. That is how your mouth gets used to the sounds, and how you get ready for real conversations.
- *If something is not clear, move on.* The point will come back, in another lesson or in a revision lesson, and the next time it will be easier.
- *Look for Spanish outside the course too:* the news, the radio, podcasts, a Spanish series with Spanish subtitles. The more you hear it, the sooner you understand it. And when a Spaniard switches to English to help you, carry on in Spanish.
- *Enjoy yourself.* Almost every dialogue ends with a joke: it is there on purpose.

## How to study a lesson

Every day the lesson guides you step by step:

1. listen to the dialogue without reading anything, repeating each sentence in the pause;

2. listen to it again while reading the English translation;

3. listen to it again while reading the Spanish text;

4. read the dialogue aloud, sentence by sentence, with the figured pronunciation and the translation beside it;

5. read the notes: they explain the new words, the grammar and the sounds;

6. do the first exercise: translating;

7. do the second: filling in the gaps;

8. read the dialogue again, trying to cover the translation;

9. listen to it one last time without the text, repeating;

10. read and listen again to the dialogues of the two previous lessons.

The number at the top of the lesson (Lección primera, Lección segunda...) is written in words and with its pronunciation: learn it every day, and by the end you will be able to count to a hundred. About half the lessons also have a cultural note, on a custom, a place or a piece of Spanish history.

In the first lessons the voice speaks a little more slowly; then it speeds up, and by about lesson 50 it reaches a natural pace. You can change the speed and the pauses in the settings.

## The figured pronunciation

Under each sentence you find how to read it, spelt as an English speaker would read it aloud: the syllable in *bold* is the one that carries the stress, in every word of two or more syllables, and a few signs show the sounds English spells differently. Every e is written é, so that it is never left silent (*lé*tché, leche); u is ou, as in soup; an s inside a word is ss, never the z of rose; H is the rough j, like the ch of Scottish loch; c before é and i, and ç elsewhere, is the th of think, as Madrid says it; gn is the ñ, like the ni of onion; tch is the ch of church; ï is the i that closes a vowel, as in hoy (oï). The Pronunciation page beside this one explains them one by one, with examples: you don't need to read it all before you start: each lesson explains the sounds it uses, and the page is there for when a sound puzzles you. Up to lesson 28 the pronunciation is given for the whole sentence; after that, only for the new words, because by then you will be able to read the ones you know on your own.

## Revision

Every seventh lesson is a revision lesson. It brings nothing new: it puts the week's grammar in order, with tables and examples you have already met, and a dialogue made of sentences from the previous lessons. Give it the same time as a new lesson: that is where the pieces fall into place.

## The two waves

The first forty-nine lessons are the *first wave*, the passive phase: you listen, repeat and understand. Your mind is working even when you feel you are doing nothing.

From lesson 50 the *second wave* begins, the active phase. Every day, after the new lesson, you go back to an old one, starting from the first: lesson 50 brings back lesson 1, lesson 51 brings back lesson 2, and so on. This time you see only the English, and you say the sentences in Spanish aloud, then compare them with the original. You will notice how far you have come.

After lesson 100 the second wave goes on, one lesson a day, until lessons 52 to 100 have been taken up again too. When the next course, Perfectionnement, is ready, you can start it without waiting for the wave to end: each of its lessons will bring back one of the last lessons of this course.

## At the end of the book

As in a book, here you also find the reference pages: a summary of the grammar, an index of grammar topics with the lessons where they are explained, the word list Spanish-English and English-Spanish, and the idioms, expressions and proverbs. Each entry tells you the lesson where you met it. You can look them up whenever you like, but remember: the course does not ask you to study them. You have already learnt them, lesson after lesson.

Y ahora... ¡adelante!

== grammar
## 1 Pronunciation, stress and the written accent

Spanish spelling is regular: once you know the rules, you can read any word aloud. The five vowels are short and pure, and they keep their sound stressed or not: an unstressed vowel is never swallowed into the "uh" of English. In Madrid the c before e and i, and the z, are the th of think (gracias, cerca, zumo); ll and y are the y of yes; the h is silent; the j, and the g before e and i, are the rough sound of Scottish loch; the ñ is the ni of onion; the single r is one tap of the tongue and the rr is rolled, as is an r at the start of a word. A u between g or q and e or i is silent (guía, aquí), and ü is sounded (pingüino).

Stress follows two rules. A word that ends in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the next-to-last syllable (*ca*sa, *ha*blan, *bue*nos); a word that ends in any other consonant is stressed on the last (Ma*drid*, ha*blar*, espa*ñol*). A written accent marks a word that breaks the rule (aquí, lección, médica). The vowels a, e and o are strong, i and u are weak: a weak vowel next to another vowel makes one syllable with it (bien, bue-nos, hay), and an accent on the weak vowel splits them (dí-as, pa-ís). The accent also tells apart words that would otherwise be spelt the same, and it is always written on question and exclamation words. Every question opens with ¿ and every exclamation with ¡. (lessons 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 14, 94)

## 2 Gender and number

Every noun is masculine or feminine, and the words around it agree. Most nouns in -o are masculine and most in -a are feminine, but there are exceptions (el programa, la radio, la mano), and nouns in -e or a consonant can be either (el café, la leche, la luz): learn each noun with its article. Nouns and adjectives of nationality add -a for a woman: inglés, inglesa. Adjectives in -o have four forms (pequeño, pequeña, pequeños, pequeñas); most others have two (grande, grandes; azul, azules), though adjectives of nationality and those in -or add -a (español, española). The plural adds -s after a vowel and -es after a consonant; a z becomes c (luz, luces; vez, veces), and a written accent may come or go to keep the stress in place (inglés, ingleses; joven, jóvenes). Words ending in an unstressed vowel and s do not change: el cumpleaños, los cumpleaños; el lunes, los lunes. A mixed group is masculine: los padres is "the parents", los hijos "the children". (lessons 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 17, 25, 26)

## 3 Articles, al and del, and lo

*Un* and *una* are "a"; the plural *unos*, *unas* is "some" or, before a number, "about" (unos ochocientos cincuenta metros). *El*, *la*, *los*, *las* are "the". A + el joins into *al* and de + el into *del*; a la and de la never join. A feminine noun that starts with a stressed a takes el in the singular (el agua, but mucha agua, las aguas). Spanish uses the article where English does not: for things in general (me gusta el cine), for days (el domingo, on Sunday), for the time (a las nueve), with señor and señora when you talk about someone, and for parts of the body and clothes, where English says "my" (me duele la cabeza). It leaves it out with a job after ser (es médica) and with otro (otro chocolate, never un otro). *Lo* with a masculine adjective makes an abstract noun: lo bueno, "the good thing"; lo mejor, "the best part". (lessons 1, 6, 10, 11, 12, 19, 20, 47, 67)

## 4 Ser and estar

Spanish has two verbs for "to be". *Ser* says what someone or something is: identity, origin, nationality, job, character, what a thing is made of, the time, the day and the date, and where an event takes place. *Estar* says where someone or something is, how someone is now, and the state a thing is in (open, closed, full); it also makes the -ing form (estoy trabajando). With a past participle, ser makes the passive (fue pintado, "was painted") and estar the resulting state (está cerrada, "it is closed"). Some adjectives change meaning with the verb. (lessons 2, 3, 4, 8, 9, 10, 24, 47, 60, 64, 98)

## 5 Pronouns and their order

The subject pronoun is usually left out, because the verb ending already says who: it is used only to stress or contrast (yo soy Javier). *Tú* is for a friend, a child, a relative or a young person; *usted* is for a stranger, an older person or a formal setting, and it takes the verb of él and ella. In Spain *vosotros* is "you all" among friends and family, and *ustedes* is "you all" in formal settings; both are explained in section 23. After a preposition the forms are mí, ti and the subject pronouns (para mí, a ti, con usted); con + mí and con + ti give *conmigo* and *contigo*.

The object pronouns come before a conjugated verb, and they are joined to the end of an infinitive, a gerund or an affirmative command (with a written accent when the stress needs it). With two verbs, either place is right: me la puedo probar, or puedo probármela. When there are two pronouns, the indirect one comes first (me lo, te la), and le or les becomes *se* before lo, la, los, las: se lo he dado. A person who is the direct object takes *a* (conozco a Pedro), and a pronoun is often doubled by a + a noun or pronoun to make clear who is meant (¿y no se lo ha dado al jefe?; a mí también). In Madrid you will often hear le for "him" as a direct object (le he visto); the Academy accepts it for a male person, and lo is never wrong. (lessons 2, 13, 15, 16, 18, 21, 22, 26, 28, 33, 48, 64, 86, 90, 98)

## 6 The present

The present has three families of verbs, by the infinitive: -ar, -er and -ir. The ending says the person, so the pronoun is left out. It is used for now, for habits, for general truths and, as in English, for a near future already arranged (nos vamos al pueblo). (lessons 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 19, 22, 33, 39, 48, 50)

| él, ella, usted | habla | come | vive |
| nosotros | hablamos | comemos | vivimos |
| vosotros | habláis | coméis | vivís |
| ellos, ellas, ustedes | hablan | comen | viven |

Many verbs change the vowel of their stem when it is stressed, that is, in every person except nosotros and vosotros: e becomes ie (empezar, empieza; querer, quiero; preferir, prefiero), o becomes ue (poder, puedo; dormir, duermo; volver, vuelvo; costar, cuesta), and jugar has u to ue (juego). A few -ir verbs change e to i (pedir, pido; vestirse, me visto; seguir, sigue). Some verbs are irregular only in the yo form: hago, pongo, salgo, traigo, conozco, sé, doy, veo. ## 7 Verb phrases: ir a, tener que, estar, llevar, seguir and others

Several verbs combine with an infinitive or a gerund to say what English says with "going to", "have to", "usually" or "-ing". *Ir a* + infinitive is the near future; *tener que* + infinitive is "to have to", and *hay que* + infinitive is "one must", with no subject; *acabar de* + infinitive is "to have just"; *volver a* + infinitive is "to do again"; *soler* + infinitive is "usually". With the gerund (-ando for -ar verbs, -iendo for -er and -ir verbs: hablando, haciendo, escribiendo; leyendo, durmiendo, pidiendo), *estar* says what is going on now, *llevar* + a time says for how long, *seguir* says that it goes on, and *ir* that it changes step by step. Spanish uses estar + gerund less than English uses "-ing": a habit takes the plain present. (lessons 9, 11, 20, 24, 37, 39, 62, 72, 80, 97)

## 8 The perfect and the preterite

The perfect is haber in the present (he, has, ha, hemos, habéis, han) and the past participle, which ends in -ado for -ar verbs and -ido for -er and -ir verbs and never changes after haber. Common irregular participles: hecho (hacer), dicho (decir), visto (ver), puesto (poner), vuelto (volver), roto (romper), escrito (escribir), abierto (abrir), muerto (morir). Nothing goes between haber and the participle: the pronouns come before haber (se lo he dado).

In Spain the choice between the two pasts follows the time. The perfect is for a time that is not over yet (hoy, esta mañana, esta semana, este año), for "ever" and "never" (alguna vez, nunca, todavía no) and for something that has just happened. The preterite is for a time that is over (ayer, el sábado, el año pasado, hace cinco años, en 1656). So a Spaniard says he dormido mal in the morning about the night just gone, where English says "I slept badly". In most of Latin America, and in Galicia, Asturias and the Canary Islands, the preterite is used for today too. (lessons 3, 18, 21, 29, 30, 31, 32, 35, 40, 42, 50, 58)

A g or c before -é is spelt gu or qu to keep its sound: llegué, toqué.

## 9 The imperfect

The imperfect is the past of description and habit: "was", "used to", "was ... -ing". Its endings are -aba for -ar verbs and -ía for -er and -ir verbs; only three verbs are irregular, ser (era), ir (iba) and ver (veía). Había is "there was" and "there were". In a story the imperfect sets the scene and says what was going on, and the preterite tells what happened: llovía mucho... ella llegó. Mientras, "while", usually takes the imperfect. The imperfect also reports what someone said in the past (dijiste que ganaba el Madrid). (lessons 17, 32, 36, 38, 42, 53, 54, 61, 97)

## 10 The pluperfect and the other compound tenses

The pluperfect is haber in the imperfect (había, habías, había, habíamos, habíais, habían) and the participle: "had done", for an event before another past event. Ya, "already", goes before the whole verb group. Haber in other tenses gives the other compound tenses: the future perfect (habrá olvidado, "will have forgotten", also a guess about the past), the conditional perfect (habría dicho, "would have said") and the pluperfect subjunctive (hubiera sabido), which work together in the si clauses of section 14. (lessons 53, 68, 72, 76)

## 11 The future and the conditional

The future adds -é, -ás, -á, -emos, -éis, -án to the whole infinitive; the conditional adds -ía, -ías, -ía, -íamos, -íais, -ían. Both use the same short stems for a few common verbs: tendr- (tener), podr- (poder), har- (hacer), dir- (decir), saldr- (salir), vendr- (venir), pondr- (poner), querr- (querer), sabr- (saber), habr- (haber). In speech, ir a + infinitive is more common than the future for plans.

The conditional is "would": it makes requests polite (¿podría...?, me gustaría, querría), gives advice, and after a past verb it reports a future (dijo que vendría). ## 12 Commands

The affirmative tú command is the él form of the present: habla, come, escucha, espera. Eight verbs are irregular: decir (di), hacer (haz), ir (ve), poner (pon), salir (sal), ser (sé), tener (ten), venir (ven). ## 13 The present subjunctive

The subjunctive is the mood of what is wished, needed, doubted or not yet real. Take the yo form of the present, drop the -o and swap the vowel of the ending: -ar verbs take e (hable, hables, hable, hablemos, habléis, hablen), -er and -ir verbs take a (coma, viva). So the irregular yo forms carry over: tengo, tenga; salgo, salga; digo, diga; conozco, conozca. Stem changes follow the present (pueda, quiera, duerma), and -ir verbs also change in nosotros and vosotros (durmamos, pidamos). Spelling keeps the sound: tocar, toque; llegar, llegue; empezar, empiece. Six verbs are irregular: ser (sea), estar (esté), ir (vaya), haber (haya), saber (sepa), dar (dé).

It follows que after a verb of wishing, asking or needing when the subject changes (quiero que empiece), after expressions of need, importance or doubt (es importante que, hace falta que, no creo que, puede que, es posible que), after ojalá, after para que, antes de que and sin que, after cuando, en cuanto and hasta que for a time still to come, after aunque for "even if", after a relative for a thing not yet known (busco un piso que tenga terraza), in wishes with que (¡que te mejores!) and in set phrases (pase lo que pase, que yo sepa). After creo que and after cuando for a habit, the indicative stays. (lessons 50, 51, 52, 54, 55, 56, 62, 66, 73, 75, 85, 89, 90, 99, 100)

## 14 The imperfect subjunctive and the si clauses

The imperfect subjunctive is made from the ellos form of the preterite: drop -ron and add -ra, -ras, -ra, -ramos, -rais, -ran, with an accent in the nosotros form (tocaron, tocara, tocáramos; tuvieron, tuviera, tuviéramos; fueron, fuera). Spain also uses a second set in -se (tocase, tuviese), with the same meaning in most uses; the lessons use the -ra forms. It follows the same triggers as the present subjunctive when the main verb is in the past or the conditional (me pidió que fuera testigo; le agradecería que me lo repitiera), and it is always used after como si, "as if". Ojalá with it is a wish that cannot come true now (ojalá fuera verdad), and quisiera is a very polite "I would like" (made example).

The pluperfect subjunctive is hubiera + participle (hubiera sabido, hubiéramos reservado). With si there are three kinds of sentence. (lessons 44, 71, 72, 74, 76, 77, 84, 98)

## 15 Por and para

## 17 Se: reflexive, impersonal, passive and involuntary

*Se* has several jobs. It is the reflexive pronoun of él, ella, usted, ellos and ustedes (se levanta, se llama), and it can mean "each other" (se conocieron, "they met"). It replaces le and les before lo, la, los, las (se lo he dado, section 5). Some verbs change meaning with it: ir, irse (to go, to leave); quedar, quedarse (to arrange to meet, to stay); poner, ponerse (to put, to become); volver, volverse (to come back, to become); comer, comerse (to eat, to eat up).

With no person named, *impersonal se* takes a singular verb: se habla inglés, "English spoken"; aquí se espera mucho, "you wait a long time here". With a thing as the subject, *passive se* makes the verb agree with it: se venden recuerdos, se alquilan bicicletas, se publicó en dos partes. *Involuntary se* says that something happened to someone without their meaning it: se me ha olvidado, "I've forgotten", literally "it has forgotten itself on me". The person is me, te, le, nos, os, les between se and the verb, and the verb agrees with the thing: se me ha roto el plato grande; se me han olvidado. (lessons 16, 57, 59, 61, 63, 64, 67, 69, 78, 82, 83, 88, 94, 97)

## 18 Relatives

*Que* is the everyday relative, for people and things: "who", "which", "that". English can leave "that" out; Spanish never leaves out que. *Quien*, *quienes* stand for people, after a preposition (a quien) or meaning "the one who, whoever" (quien mira el cuadro; hay quien...). *El que*, *la que*, *los que*, *las que* are "the one(s) who, the one(s) that" and agree with what they stand for; *lo que* is "what, the thing that". *El cual*, *la cual* belong to formal language, often after a preposition; *lo cual* points back to a whole statement ("which"). *Cuyo*, "whose", agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner (cuyo piso, cuya plaza). *Donde* is "where". After a relative the verb is in the indicative for a thing that is known, and in the subjunctive for a thing not yet known or found. (lessons 64, 66, 67, 69, 80, 82, 84, 88, 95, 97)

## 19 Comparatives and superlatives, -ísimo

*Más ... que* is "more ... than" or "-er than", and *menos ... que* "less ... than"; before a number, "than" is de (más de doscientos kilómetros). *Tan ... como* is "as ... as", and *tanto*, *tanta*, *tantos*, *tantas* ... como is "as much, as many ... as". Four adjectives have their own forms: mejor (better), peor (worse), mayor (older, bigger) and menor (younger, smaller); no más goes before them. ## 20 Diminutives and augmentatives

The diminutive makes a thing small, and much more often makes it friendly, warm or polite: un momentito, "just a moment"; un cafelito, "a nice little coffee". The usual ending is -ito, -ita, after dropping a final -o or -a (rato, ratito; momento, momentito); words ending in -e, -n or -r take -cito, -cita (café, cafecito, or cafelito in Madrid speech), and short words, or words with ie or ue, take -ecito, -ecita (siesta, siestecita). It can go even on adverbs: ahora mismito, "right this minute". Each region has its favourite: -illo in Andalusia, -ico in Aragon, Navarre and Murcia, -ín in Asturias. The augmentatives -ón, -ona and -azo make a thing big (un portón, un cochazo), and -azo can also mean a blow with something: sartenazo, a blow with a frying pan. (lessons 34, 86, 91)

## 21 Numbers, dates and the time

From 16 to 29 numbers are written as one word (dieciséis, veintiuno, veintidós, veintiséis), from 31 with y (treinta y uno, cuarenta y cinco). Uno becomes un before a masculine noun and una before a feminine one (veintiún años, veintiuna personas). Cien is a hundred on its own and before a noun or mil; above it, ciento (ciento uno, ciento veinte). The hundreds agree with the noun (doscientos euros, doscientas personas); note quinientos, setecientos and novecientos. Mil does not change (dos mil), and millón takes de before a noun (un millón de euros). Years are read as whole numbers: mil novecientos veintiséis, dos mil veinticuatro. Spain writes a thousand as 1.000 and decimals with a comma: 2,5.

The ordinals are primero, segundo, tercero, cuarto, quinto, sexto, séptimo, octavo, noveno, décimo; primero and tercero drop the -o before a masculine noun (el primer piso). A floor of a building is often just el quinto, el segundo. Dates use cardinal numbers: el veintinueve de febrero, el uno de marzo (in Latin America also el primero). Days and months take no capital letter, and "on" a day is el (el domingo; los domingos, on Sundays).

The time takes ser and the article: es la una, son las dos. Minutes are added with y up to the half hour (y cuarto, y media, y diez) and taken away with menos after it (las nueve menos cuarto). "At" is a: a las nueve, a la una. De la mañana, de la tarde and de la noche give the part of the day; en punto is "sharp", a mediodía "at midday" and a medianoche "at midnight". Timetables use the 24-hour clock. (lessons 4, 7, 9, 13, 14, 25, 28, 37, 64, 72, 74, 79, 80, 82, 92, 100)

## 22 Negatives and questions

No goes before the verb, and the object pronouns stay between it and the verb (no lo sé). A second negative word after the verb does not cancel the no, so the double negative is correct: no he visto a nadie, no hay nada. Placed before the verb, the negative word stands alone: nadie lo entiende (made example); ¿nunca sale de la portería?. The pairs are alguien and nadie, algo and nada, alguno and ninguno (algún, ningún before a masculine noun), siempre and nunca, también and tampoco. A question can keep the order of a statement, with the voice rising; ¿no? or ¿verdad? at the end asks for a yes. Question words carry an accent, also in indirect questions: qué, quién, cuál, cómo, dónde, adónde, cuándo, cuánto, por qué (the answer porque has none). ## 23 Tú, usted, vosotros, and Spain and Latin America
