== intro
## What Mastery is for

With Perfectionnement you reached C1: you understand nearly everything that is said and written in Spain, and you say what you mean. This book takes you from C1 to C2, where the question is no longer "is it correct?" but "is it the right word, in the right register, with the effect I wanted?". Classic self-study courses stop at C1. Mastery is Lingua Onde's own sequel, and it keeps the heart of the method: one long text a day, heard and read closely, the notes, the translation, the second wave and a revision every seven lessons. What changes is what you are asked to do. Understanding is no longer enough: you have to notice what a text does, and be able to do it yourself.

## Who it is for

For anyone who has finished Perfectionnement, and for anyone who already reads Spanish without effort and wants to hear its shades: the usted that slips out at a family dinner, the es que that announces an excuse, the a ver si quedamos that means you will not meet, the praise that is really a verdict, the verb of saying that takes sides, the exact word where an almost right one would do.

## How it works

Each lesson is a long text complete in itself, of 25 to 40 sentences, read at natural speed. The kind changes every day: an essay, a letter, a column, a speech, a story, a poem, a set of statutes, a lecture, a debate in the Congreso de los Diputados, the transcript of two friends talking in a kitchen. Each week has a focus of style, and its six texts practise it from six sides.

The fourteen weeks go in this order: register, from the bar on the corner to the ministry; saying less, with the small words that soften and the irony that says the opposite; persuasion, from the pregón of a village fiesta to a debating club; telling a story; the precise word; work and the law, from statutes to a will at the notary's; the language of knowledge, from the lecture to the thesis defence. Then the varieties: Andalusia and the Canaries, Mexico, the River Plate, the Caribbean and the Andes, and Spanish beside Catalan, Galician and Basque. Then humour, from stand-up to the chirigota and the greguería; verse and sound; argument, Parliament and the press; real speech, with its fillers, its swearing and its slang; tact, from condolence to apology. The last week follows Spanish through time, from the Glosas Emilianenses to a merchant's letter of 1605 and the Spanish of Sefarad, and between English and Spanish: Spanglish, calques and a translator's diary. Two lessons close the course.

The notes deal with register, what is meant but not said, irony, cultural and historical references, idioms and fixed pairs, the exact word against its near-synonyms, and the contrasts with English. Foundation and Perfectionnement taught the grammar: where it is needed, the notes send you back to them. Exercise 1 asks you to translate sentences where the nuance matters; exercise 2, to fill in idioms, collocations and connectors.

After the exercises come one or two open tasks: summarise a text, change its register, answer a column, write a resignation letter, a toast, eight lines of comic verse, a condolence message. There is no single right answer. Write your own (or say it aloud), then set it beside a model answer and a short self-check list: meaning, register, structure, idiom, effect. The model shows one good way to do it, not the only one.

Every seventh lesson is a revision: a new text that brings back the week's means, with references to the lessons, and notes that put them in order in tables. At the end of the book you find, once the course is finished, the style and grammar appendix, the index of topics and the expressions.

## Tips for use

*The first wave.* Every day, about three quarters of an hour:

1. listen to the text without reading, for its tone;

2. read it in Spanish, with the notes, and look at the English translation only where you need it;

3. listen to it again while reading;

4. read aloud the passages you like best, until they sound like yours;

5. do the two exercises;

6. do the task, and only then look at the model answer and the self-check list.

*The notes.* They no longer explain words one by one: they explain choices. Why fallecer and not morir, why usted here and tú there, why a short sentence, what a Spanish reader understands that an English one misses. Read them even when the text seems clear: that is often where the point is.

*The tasks.* Write first, then compare. Go through the self-check list line by line: has the meaning survived, is the register the one asked for, does the text have the shape of its kind (a letter opens and closes like a letter), are the idioms used in their real sense, does it have the effect it should? Where the model does something you did not, try it in your next task, not in this one.

*The Mastery wave.* As lesson 49 tells you, the wave starts at lesson 50: lesson 50 brings back lesson 1, lesson 51 brings back lesson 2, and so on, one old lesson a day after the new one. This time you read the text in English and rebuild it in Spanish, whole: the same meaning, the same tone, the same effect, not word for word. Then you set your version beside the original and check it with the list. If a whole text is too much, go sentence by sentence. After lesson 100 the wave goes on, as the letter of lesson 99 says: every day you rebuild an old lesson, until lesson 100 has come back too. If you come from Perfectionnement, its own second wave goes on meanwhile: the first lessons of this course each bring back, one a day, the last lessons of Perfectionnement not yet taken up again.

*The revision lessons.* They have no exercises: a text and a task. They put the week's style in order, so that you recognise it the next time you meet it outside the course, in a paper, at a meeting or at a market stall.

*The appendix.* The style and grammar sections take up, week by week, what the notes and the revisions put in order, with examples from the lessons and the lesson's number; they point back to Foundation and Perfectionnement instead of repeating them. The index leads from a topic to the lessons that deal with it; the expressions give the sense and the lessons where you met them, the first one first. They are for finding things again, not for learning by heart.

Ready? Then... ¡adelante!

== grammar
## 1 Register: tú, usted, vosotros and ustedes

Foundation (lessons 13 and 93) and Perfectionnement (lesson 29) set out who says tú and who says usted in Spain. Mastery looks at what a choice says and what a switch signals. Tú is now the form for almost everyone, from the bank's app to the supermarket receipt and the energy company; usted stays in official letters, in some neighbourhood shops, with the very old and in the mouths of some very young waiters, and the first usted you receive tells you your age. Tú is offered (trátame de tú) and can be refused politely (Como usted quiera, caballero); a habit of usted can survive fifteen years in a family and give way, at last, in a toast. With usted the same formula changes its pronoun: lo miro y te digo to a friend, lo miro y le digo in an office. In Spain vosotros is the plural of tú and ustedes the plural of usted; in most of Andalusia, in the Canaries and in Latin America ustedes is the only plural, so the Madrileña back from Gran Canaria who asks her parents ¿Ustedes qué quieren cenar? sounds as if she spoke to them de usted. The vet who tells a family bad news speaks to them as ustedes; a translator has to choose among the four forms every time English says "you"; and vuestra merced, which took a verb in the third person, is the ancestor of usted. ## 2 Saying less: softeners, hedges and the polite no

A Spanish request is short. Ponme una caña at a bar or ¿Me pasas la sal? at the table is perfectly polite in Spain, and an English ear hears an order; the English chain of formulas, said in Spanish, sounds cold or over-elaborate. Spanish softens with small words, not with long ones: es que announces an excuse, which comes next; a ver asks for time before an answer; bueno can agree, give in or close a conversation without closing it; igual means "perhaps" in Spain; un poco and un momentito (the -ito of Foundation, lesson 86) lower a request, and a momentito has no fixed length. The conditional asks about the other's willingness (¿Te importaría...?, ¿Podrías...?), and the present asks as if the thing were already settled (¿Me dejas...?). Un poco can say a great deal: estoy un poco cansada may mean "I am not coming out". A no is said without no: a ver si quedamos is a courtesy with the date left blank, ya te diré promises an answer that rarely comes, lo miro y te digo sounds like paperwork, and me pilla fatal is the excuse of someone who has no better one, in that order from the softest to the most direct. Bad news is made small in the same way (nada importante, un poco, tampoco es para tanto), and the listener hears the warning. Mexico softens further than Spain: ¿me da un café?, ¿me podría traer un café? (lessons 8, 14, 20, 21, 52, 82, 84)

## 3 Hyperbole, irony and the straight face

Where English understates, Spanish exaggerates, and nobody takes it literally: mil veces, nos comieron vivos, se moría de calor, una eternidad, ¡Está hirviendo!, colorados como cangrejos. Told in Spanish without one exaggeration, the same holiday sounds cold, not modest. Irony in praise says the opposite with good manners: the review of lesson 10 finds the idea ingenious, no dish wrong and the wine correct, sin más, and keeps its warmest praise for the bread, which is not on the menu. The ritual formulas of a visit to a newborn (¡Enhorabuena!, ¡Qué rico!, ¡Es clavadita a su padre!, ¡Qué bueno es!) are not judgements, and the parents' plain answers keep them polite until the last line gives the truth away. Deadpan satire borrows a register and applies it to something trivial: the policy paper of lesson 12 taxes the sobremesa by the hour, with exemptions (Swift's A Modest Proposal is named in its note), and the news story of lesson 62 creates a committee to study the creation of committees. Mock outrage uses every formula of the letter to the editor for a bowl of olives, and the postscript gives it away. (lessons 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 62, 63)

## 4 Persuasion and rhetoric

The pregón that opens a village's fiestas uses the oldest tools of the speech: the apostrophe to the town (¡Vecinos de Cerrollano!), the anaphora (vosotros, los que...; Aquí se aprendió...), the list of three that builds to the last item, the rhetorical question, the closing cry, and the promise to be brief that a speaker never keeps. Advertising speaks to you with tú and the imperative, which fakes a closeness that does not exist; it uses a superlative with nothing to compare it with, por fin, three sin that say what is not there and keep quiet about what is, and hasta in its strongest sense; the sceptic's notes of lesson 16 take each device apart. In a debate a point is granted before it is turned: es cierto que ... pero, si bien, aun reconociendo que, por mucho que with the subjunctive (Perfectionnement, section 1 of its appendix), and lo de + noun takes up the other side's point; the chair has formulas of its own (Se abre la sesión, tienes la palabra, cierra el turno). The texts that made a country (the Constitution of 1812, la Pepa; that of 1978; the King's message on the night of the 23-F) are told in a solemn register, with the past for events and the present for what still holds. At the door, the seller's devices (es solo un momento, la oferta acaba hoy) meet a grandmother who turns each one round. (lessons 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 21)

## 5 Telling a story

Perfectionnement (section 5 of its appendix) set out the tenses of a story. Here they are choices. The present puts the reader on the night bus beside the narrator, and the past comes in only where someone remembers (Yo trabajé muchos años enfrente); como si with the imperfect subjunctive compares with what is not true. The costumbrista sketch of lesson 23, in the manner of the 1830s (Larra's «Vuelva usted mañana» is named in its note), presents its man in one long sentence of asides and relatives before he moves, with period words in modern spelling. A memory works with the imperfect of habit and the senses (the botijo, the siesta behind closed shutters, tomar el fresco on chairs outside the door), and a preterite closes it: La casa se vendió hace años. The microrrelato (Monterroso's «El dinosaurio» is named in a note) leaves the reader the why, the when and the who, and lets one verb carry the story. Free indirect style lets a woman's mind speak in the narrator's sentence, without quotation marks and without pensó (Clarín's La Regenta is named in a note): an exclamation, a question, tal vez with the subjunctive. The unreliable narrator is corrected by his family in round brackets, and his claim shrinks until the truth comes out. (lessons 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28)

## 6 The precise word: synonyms, false friends and the verbs of saying

Near-synonyms differ by register, and the register says who is speaking and where. Dying has a verb for every occasion: morir is neutral; fallecer belongs to notices and official texts; perecer to accidents and disasters; fenecer is old and written (a deadline can fenecer); irse and dejarnos speak of leaving rather than dying; pasar a mejor vida is a formula of consolation, often ironic; estirar la pata and irse al otro barrio are familiar, palmarla rougher, criar malvas the most ironic. Mixing them is the mistake: un «ha palmado» en una esquela. The editor's red pencil prefers the plain verb (hacer to realizar) and cuts the empty phrases a nivel de, en base a, de cara a and poner en valor, and el mismo used as a pronoun. The verbs of saying take sides: decir is neutral; afirmar states firmly; asegurar wants to be believed and sostener holds out against objections; alegar gives a reason, often an excuse; admitir concedes reluctantly and reconocer concedes; confesar reveals what was hidden; señalar and comentar have the tone of a report; murmurar is said low and with bad intent; espetar and soltar are sudden. Foundation (lesson 96) and Perfectionnement (section 17 of its appendix) taught the basic false friends; the advanced ones of lesson 33 deceive those who think they know: compromiso, eventual, soportar, suceso, introducir, pretender, discusión, tópico, ignorar, argumento, educado, comprensivo, en absoluto. And the right word, sometimes, was in the first draft. (lessons 29, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 100)

## 7 Cause and consequence

Four words sound alike. Porque, in one word, gives the cause; por qué, in two, asks the question, with an accent in a direct or an indirect question (no sé por qué se enfada); el porqué is a noun, "the reason", with a plural los porqués; por que, rare, is por with que (luchamos por que nos paguen, el motivo por que se fue). Ask whether you can put an article before it: if you can, it is el porqué. Among the words of cause, ya que sounds natural in conversation, puesto que belongs mostly to writing and dado que to reports and documents; como gives a cause above all at the start of its sentence. Before a noun, debido a and a causa de are neutral, gracias a gives a good cause and por culpa de a bad one. The consequence follows with así que in conversation, por eso in any register, por tanto in writing and de ahí before a noun; Perfectionnement (section 10 of its appendix) taught por consiguiente and así pues. The reader's reply of lesson 30 uses every word correctly, to complain. (lessons 1, 2, 8, 12, 30, 35, 43)

## 8 Letters, emails and the office

Perfectionnement (section 13 of its appendix) set out the instancia and the town hall's reply. Here the same writer changes register with the reader: the message to a friend has tía, flipar and es que, and the email to the managing agent opens with Estimado señor and a colon, states the facts, asks with le ruego que and closes with quedo a la espera and un cordial saludo. The saluda in a fiesta programme piles up the formulas of public life (Queridos vecinos, me dirijo a todos ustedes, es para mí un honor, no puedo por menos que lamentar, aprovecho la ocasión) until the postscript says the plain fact. The letter to the editor opens with Señor director and closes with Gracias de antemano por su atención and Atentamente. The perfect resignation letter names the rule it follows (con el preaviso establecido en mi contrato) and thanks for the trust, not for the work. The yearly review softens every criticism (áreas de mejora, salir de tu zona de confort) and closes with formulas that commit to nothing: ¿tú cómo lo ves?, te escucho, me lo apunto. The email after a meeting presents a decision as agreed (según lo acordado) and asks with the subjunctive (les ruego que envíen), and the colleagues' WhatsApp chat tells the same meeting in another tone, until one of them answers the email, to everyone, in the tone of the chat. ## 9 Statutes, wills and the law

Perfectionnement's appeal against a fine (section 13 of its appendix: Visto, Considerando, Procédase) showed the administration's style. Statutes speak in articles, in the future of obligation (quedarán obligados, abonarán, decidirá) and in short formulas, each with its job: salvo before a noun, sin perjuicio de, en su caso, a tal efecto. The last article, on all that the statutes do not foresee, is the only one that matters, as the commentary in plain Spanish says. At the notary's the law of inheritance is explained in conversation: under the Código Civil that applies in most of Spain two thirds of an estate are reserved for the children (la legítima), and one third can be left freely (el tercio de libre disposición); heredero, legado, usufructo and albacea each have their exact sense, and several regions, Galicia and Cataluña among them, have their own civil law. The portrait of Concepción Arenal separates the story from the record with según se cuenta, and ends with her maxim. The policy paper of lesson 12 borrows the same register for satire. ## 10 The language of knowledge

The popular lecture guides its audience: it asks leave before an image (si me permiten una imagen), restates the technical point plainly (dicho en términos sencillos), expects the room's question (ustedes me dirán) and points at the slide (miren, por ejemplo). The scientific article never says "we show": each hedge keeps a claim at the level it can bear, from los resultados sugieren and parece indicar to cabe pensar, no puede descartarse and se requieren más estudios. Spanish academic prose hides its author, where English writes "I": the plural of modesty (creemos, hemos comprobado) for one writer, the passive with se, whose verb agrees with its subject (se recogieron las muestras), the impersonal se with a singular verb (se trabaja), and the noun style (la recogida de muestras, el análisis de los datos), which gains density and loses people. A thesis defence has its tribunal (presidente, secretario, vocal) and its formulas, from se abre el acto to tomo nota and hemos terminado las preguntas. Popular science explains against a reference: heights in Spain are measured from the mean sea level at Alicante, and roads count from the kilómetro cero in the Puerta del Sol. A review balances its verdict: what the book does well, what se echa en falta, and a recommendation with a warning. (lessons 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49)

## 11 One language, many voices

Foundation (lesson 94) heard the Andalusian accent, and Perfectionnement portrayed the Canaries, Mexico, the River Plate and the Caribbean as places (lessons 30, 37, 43 and 50) and drew the map in section 18 of its appendix. Mastery listens to the way they speak, and each word is named with its place and its Madrid equivalent. Andalusia: the seseo (cierra and sierra sound alike), and in parts of Cádiz and Málaga the opposite, an s that sounds like the c of cielo in Madrid; the s at the end of a syllable breathed or dropped; the d of -ado lost in conversation, which Madrid does too among friends; ustedes in the place of vosotros in most of Andalusia; illo and quillo in Seville; and hablar fino, said of someone who speaks like the north, and not always as praise. The Canaries: guagua, papas arrugadas con mojo, millo, mi niña, ¡chacho!, the seseo, ustedes for vosotros, and the silbo of La Gomera. Mexico: ¿mande?, ahorita, platicar, the camión that is a bus, la chamba, padrísimo, ¡órale!, güey between friends, and requests softer than Madrid's. The River Plate: the voseo (vos sos, devolvés; vení, sentate, probá), che, dale, laburo, quilombo, re, boludo between friends, and the mate passed round by the cebador, where each gracias means "no more". The Caribbean: asere, the pronoun before the verb in a question (¿qué tú quieres?); the Andes: nomás, ¡achachay!, the diminutive everywhere, and Quechua words (choclo, cancha, and guagua for a baby). Beside Catalan, Galician and Basque, Spanish takes touches of each: a final d said as t (Madrit), plegar for leaving work, the simple past where Madrid uses the perfect (¿comiste ya?), -iño, morriña, aita, ama, agur and kaixo. Madrid has its own: the laísmo of la dije, which the grandchildren correct together. To speak and write, use the Spanish of Spain; to listen, keep every ear open. ## 12 Humour and wordplay

Stand-up lives on timing: a list of small facts sets up an ordinary life, a pattern of three puts the joke in the middle, and the last line lands on what the build-up hid (Es mi madre). Parody copies the form of the official test, with its usted instructions, answers that are little scenes and a close that is pure bureaucracy; the real test for nationality, the CCSE, is named in a note. The chirigota of the Cádiz carnival teases its victim in octosyllable stanzas with a chorus the audience takes home, a costume and a pun (subir: the lift that does not go up, the fee that does). The absurd sketch follows a rule to the letter until it produces its own result: an appointment to ask for an appointment. The greguería of Ramón Gómez de la Serna is, in his words, humour plus metaphor: an everyday object seen as if for the first time, with a turn at the end, and the best one of the week is an eight-year-old's. Satire in news register reports an absurd plan with every formula of the local paper (por unanimidad, salió adelante, la abstención de la oposición) and no comment at all. ## 13 Verse and sound

Perfectionnement (section 19 of its appendix) taught how to count syllables: the sinalefa, the last-stress rule and assonance. Mastery uses them. The romance has lines of eight syllables, assonance in the even lines and the odd lines free, like a conversation; in an assonance only the stressed vowel and the last one count (callada, rama, nada). The poet of lesson 64 read Machado's Campos de Castilla before he began. Bécquer's Rimas, published in 1871 after his death, answer the question "what is poetry?" with «Poesía... eres tú», which defines nothing and stays in the memory; Rubén Darío's modernismo, with Azul... (1888), brought music, colour and new metres such as the alejandrino. The copla of the 1940s and 1950s has stanzas of four octosyllables, a full (consonant) rhyme in the second and fourth lines, and a refrain the audience sings without having learnt it. Twin formulas live on sound: a trancas y barrancas, contra viento y marea, sano y salvo, sin ton ni son, erre que erre, and slogans borrow them. Free verse breaks the line where the breath stops (Dámaso Alonso's Hijos de la ira, 1944, is named), and reading poetry on the radio means letting the sounds come before the sense; the radio poem is in the manner of Rosalía de Castro, who wrote in Galician and in Spanish. ## 14 Argument, Parliament and the press

An editorial speaks with the paper's own voice (Este periódico...), names a change as a thing (el reparto del espacio), grants the other side's argument before it turns it, and gives its verdict in a short sentence. Fallacies have Spanish names, and naming one brings a discussion back to the point: el hombre de paja, la pendiente resbaladiza, el razonamiento circular, el ataque personal, el falso dilema. In the Congreso de los Diputados a question opens with Señora presidenta, señorías, señor ministro; the minister thanks the Member for the question and says nothing that can be checked (la materia no admite atajos), and the actas keep what was said. A government is formed by investiture: the King consults the parties' representatives and proposes a candidate; the first vote needs an absolute majority (at least 176 of the 350 deputies), and a second vote forty-eight hours later needs more yes than no votes; if nobody is invested within two months of the first vote, there are new elections. The words of compromise are acuerdo, abstención, línea roja and apoyos puntuales, and the ministers promise or swear their office. A radio tertulia shows evasion at length, and the presenter closes on it; two columns answer the same question, one balanced and one partisan. (lessons 62, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77)

## 15 Real speech: markers, small answers, swearing and slang

Speech is not broken writing. Perfectionnement (section 11 of its appendix) taught the small words of conversation; the transcript of lesson 78 shows them at work, with interruptions and sentences left unfinished: o sea narrows or corrects, en plan softens before the verb, digamos hedges a word, total sums up, pues nada closes a subject that went nowhere, ¿sabes? asks for attention. The small answers carry the meaning: ya, ya agrees without listening, vale agrees and moves on, venga closes a point, anda stops a remark, hombre protests gently, ¡qué va! denies, vaya lío sums up, ¿eh? and ¿no? ask for agreement, and que repeats with insistence (que no, papá). Swearing is common in Spain and says more about register than about anger; the soft forms jolín, jope, ostras, caray, mecachis and miércoles are safe, the strongest words are only named, and coger, ordinary in Madrid, is vulgar in Mexico and much of Argentina, where you say tomar el autobús. Voicemails show self-repair and the hedge that warns (nada importante, un poco), and the market stall has its patter, with usted, the price without an article and an exclamation for everything. ## 16 Tact: condolence, farewell, bad news and apology

A death has its formulas: mi más sentido pésame, said to the family, and te acompaño en el sentimiento, said whole; the esquela in the paper ends with descanse en paz, and friends go to the tanatorio. A good letter of condolence uses the formulas as a frame for something concrete, refuses the platitude (el tiempo lo cura todo) and ends warmly. A eulogy can make people smile with concrete memories, and the old pupils meet again over coffee after the funeral. Bad news is given early and plainly, with the apology first (lo siento, pero no), praises the family for coming, softens only the hardest word (un medicamento que lo duerme sin dolor) and leaves the decision to those who must take it. Lo siento expresses regret, perdón, perdona and perdone ask forgiveness, disculpe excuses, lamento is formal regret, and pedir perdón must be earned; a real apology says what happened, and the non-apology regrets that alguien se haya sentido ofendido, hiding both the offender and the offence. Some words need a sentence in English: sobremesa, estrenar, vergüenza ajena. A retirement speech teases the colleague who is leaving with his own favourite phrase, thanks him for what no file records, and ends by filing itself: eso también estará en el legajo. ## 17 Spanish through time

From the spoken Latin of the Empire, region by region, came the Romance languages. The Glosas Emilianenses, notes in the margin of a Latin manuscript of San Millán de la Cogolla, are traditionally counted among the first written traces of Romance; the Cantar de mio Cid survives in a single manuscript copied by Per Abbat; Alfonso X the Wise, in the 13th century, gave Castilian the work of Latin: law, history and science. Al-Andalus left an Arabic layer of everyday words (aceite, azúcar, almohada, azotea, ajedrez, and with the article al- alcalde, albañil, aldea), and ojalá, "if God wills", said every day without a thought of God. In 1492 Nebrija published his Gramática de la lengua castellana, one of the first grammars of a modern European language, and called the language compañera del imperio; the Real Academia Española, founded in 1713, took the motto Limpia, fija y da esplendor and published the Diccionario de autoridades (1726–1739); the Ortografía of 2010 stopped counting ch and ll as letters and named the y ye; the Association of Academies dates from 1951. A merchant's letter of 1605, in modern spelling, keeps the forms of its time: vuestra merced with a verb in the third person, con esta mía, loado sea Dios, agora, ansí, mesmo, harto, and the closing beso las manos de vuestra merced. The Jews expelled in 1492 took their Castilian to Salonica, Istanbul, Sarajevo and northern Morocco (haketía); ladino, strictly, is the language of word-for-word translations of sacred texts; for some five centuries it was spoken at home, and it is learnt by singing. ## 18 Between English and Spanish: Spanglish, calques and translation
