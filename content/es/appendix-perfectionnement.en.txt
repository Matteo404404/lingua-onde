== intro
## What Perfectionnement is for

This book sets out to take your Spanish from the level where it works to the level where it sounds right: from B2 to C1. Beyond the language, it takes you on a journey through the world that speaks it: Andalusia, Galicia, Catalonia, the Basque Country and the Canary Islands, then Mexico, the River Plate, the Caribbean and the Spanish of the United States, and Madrid at the end; painters, writers and figures of the past, from Velázquez and Goya to Frida Kahlo, from Cervantes to Lorca, Machado, Borges and Neruda, and Almodóvar's cinema; and everyday life, from two friends fighting to pay for the coffees to the long weekend, the in-laws, the driving test, a book club, an appeal against a fine and an oral exam. All of it with plenty of humour, because the way a people jokes says a great deal about the way it thinks.

## Who it is for

For anyone who has finished Foundation, but also for anyone who already has a good level of Spanish and wants to go deeper: the commonest words with all their idioms (echar, quedar, dar, poner, llevar, pasar, sacar, tirar and cara), the subjunctive where English has none, the small words that set the tone of a conversation, the constructions you know but still use badly, register, from a friend's tú to the town hall's instancia, and the Spanish of Mexico, Buenos Aires and Havana beside that of Madrid.

## How it works

Like every Assimil course: texts, notes that explain the grammar and the vocabulary, and exercises. Each lesson is a text complete in itself, and the kind changes from day to day: a dialogue, the portrait of a place, a story, a satire, a news bulletin, a poem, a letter, a life, three jokes, a grandmother who answers only in proverbs. About every eight lessons, one lesson is given to a single word and its idioms, and its notes end with the list of its expressions. About every seven, a portrait follows the same plan: where the place is and what it is like, its towns, a moment of its history, its landscape, what it makes, what it eats and drinks, and a last line on a speciality.

Every seventh lesson is a revision lesson that puts the finer points of the week in order, in tables. At the end of the book you find the grammar appendix, the index of topics and the expressions: look them up whenever you need to. After lesson 70 comes Mastery, the next course: longer texts and, at the end of each lesson, a task in which you write.

## Tips for use

*The first wave.* Every day, half an hour:

1. listen to the text;

2. read it in Spanish, with the help of the notes and, when you need it, the English translation;

3. listen to it again;

4. read each sentence aloud, then repeat it without looking, until it comes naturally;

5. do the two exercises, translating and filling in;

6. listen to the text one last time.

*The notes.* They explain the new words and constructions, but they also widen your vocabulary with other words and idioms around the one in the text, and they often send you back to a lesson of Foundation or to an earlier lesson of this course. Read them even when the text seems easy. The figured pronunciation has gone: you read Spanish spelling, which you know by now. Where a sound matters, a foreign name, the x of México, a stress that changes the meaning (término, termino, terminó), the note gives it with the signs of Foundation's key (the Pronunciation page of the courses).

*The second wave.* At lesson 50, start again from the first: lesson 50 brings back lesson 1, lesson 51 brings back lesson 2, and so on. Every day, after the new lesson, you take up an old one, and this time you translate the text from English into Spanish, then check. After lesson 70 the wave goes on, one lesson a day, until lesson 70 has come back too. If you come from Foundation, its own second wave goes on meanwhile: lessons 1 to 49 of this course each bring back, one a day, the last lessons of Foundation not yet taken up again, up to lesson 100. If you start here directly, the second wave simply begins at lesson 50.

*The revision lessons.* They do not repeat the basic grammar, which you already know: they sharpen the points where even good speakers still go wrong, and they end with ten sentences to translate and to fill in.

Ready? Then... ¡manos a la obra!

== grammar
## 1 The subjunctive where English has none

Foundation taught the present and imperfect subjunctive and their usual triggers (Foundation, lessons 50 to 56, 66, 71 to 77 and 85). This course adds the cases where English uses a plain verb, so the mood has to come from the meaning. A feeling or a judgement about a whole clause takes que and the subjunctive: le molestaba que, no soportaba que, el hecho de que (with el hecho de que the indicative is also heard for a plain fact). In Spain extrañar means "to surprise", and it takes the subjunctive like molestar. Antes de que and sin que always take it, and so does a relative about someone or something not known (el que organice, nada que yo no haya visto). Asking, recommending, advising and wishing for another person take que and the subjunctive (pidió que escribiera, te recomiendo que leas, te deseo que tengas), and so do desmentir que and the forecasts se espera que and se prevé que.

Some forms leave the choice open, repeat the verb or concede. Lo que tú digas, como quieras, cuando puedas and donde prefieras leave the decision to the other person, and in conversation they can mean the opposite of what they say. The reduplicated form repeats the verb around que: llegue el puente que llegue, sea como sea, pase lo que pase (Foundation, lesson 85), "whatever". Aunque with the indicative states a fact, "although"; with the subjunctive it gives a case that may or may not happen, or one the speaker will not argue about, "even if"; aunque sea is "even if only". Por mucho que always takes the subjunctive, whatever the time. No es que with the subjunctive denies a reason, and es que with the indicative gives the real one. Que yo recuerde, like que yo sepa, limits a statement to what the speaker knows. (lessons 8, 10, 13, 14, 17, 19, 26, 36, 38, 44, 46, 51, 69)

## 2 Wishes, formulas and irony

Some subjunctives have frozen into formulas: they no longer change, and English keeps a few of the same kind (long live the King, be that as it may, God save the King). Que with the subjunctive is the wish of Foundation, lesson 90, said at table, at a goodbye or after a death; ¡vaya!, ¡venga! and ¡viva! are old subjunctives too. Ojalá with the imperfect subjunctive wishes for what cannot come true now (Foundation, lesson 72). Quién with the imperfect subjunctive, as in ¡quién pudiera!, is "if only I could", and ¡si al menos ...! with the imperfect subjunctive and no main clause is "if only, at least". Irony turns the words round. ¡Qué bien!, said with a tight smile, means the opposite; ¡Menudo ...! and ¡Vaya ...! are "what a ...", often for something big or annoying; no está mal can praise or complain, and only the tone tells you which. (lessons 31, 35, 66, 69, 70)

## 3 Conditions and their mixed forms

Foundation gave the three kinds of si clause (Foundation, lessons 44, 71 and 76). Si never takes the present subjunctive or the future. The mixed kind joins a past that did not happen to a result now: si with the pluperfect subjunctive, then the conditional (si hubiera negociado así, ahora tendría). De with haber and the participle replaces si with the pluperfect subjunctive: de haberlo sabido, "had I known". Como with the subjunctive at the start of the sentence is a warning or a threat. The other conditions take the subjunctive: siempre que, "provided that" (with the indicative it means "whenever"); con tal de que, firmer, the terms of a deal; a no ser que and salvo que, "unless", salvo que for a rule with an exception; en caso de que, "in the event that"; suponiendo que and the formal en el supuesto de que, "assuming that". (lessons 22, 26, 28, 68, 70)

## 4 Probability, deduction and the modal past

From the likeliest to the least likely: lo más seguro es que and se prevé que take the subjunctive; a lo mejor always takes the indicative; quizá, quizás and tal vez take either mood, the subjunctive for what is still open; igual, familiar in Spain, usually takes the indicative; puede que takes the subjunctive. To deduce, deber de with an infinitive is a likely guess, tener que the only conclusion the evidence allows, and no puede ser the impossible; deber without de is an obligation (debe cuidar). The conditional guesses about the past as the future does about the present (Foundation, lesson 68): tendría unos veinte, "she would have been about twenty". For "should have", "needn't have" and "could have" Spanish says tendría que haber with the participle, or debiste with the infinitive; no hacía falta que with the imperfect subjunctive, or no tenías por qué with the infinitive; and podrías haber with the participle. The pronouns join haber: habértelo, haberlo. (lessons 10, 13, 14, 27, 28, 38, 42)

## 5 The tenses of a story, the future in the past and a life

Foundation set out the preterite, the imperfect and the pluperfect (the Foundation book, sections 8 to 10). In a story the preterite moves the events on, the imperfect paints the scene and the habit, and the pluperfect goes back to what had already happened. Four verbs change their English in the preterite: supo, "found out"; conoció, "met"; no quiso, "refused"; pudo, "managed". Justo cuando with the imperfect sets the moment another event broke in, and nada más with an infinitive and apenas are "as soon as" and "hardly". Seen from a past moment, the future becomes the conditional (sería), and a plan becomes iba a with an infinitive; estuvo a punto de says it nearly happened and did not, acabó with a gerund what came instead, llegó a ser what someone became in the end. The future perfect, habrás leído, is what will be done by a time to come. A biography adds tras with a noun, al cabo de with a period, una vez with a participle and hasta que with the preterite, and it keeps the past for the life and the present for what still holds today. History names its events with nouns made from verbs: aprobar gives la aprobación, legalizar la legalización (-ción and -dad are feminine, -miento and -aje masculine). (lessons 2, 5, 6, 7, 16, 32, 36, 39, 42, 44, 49)

## 6 Reported speech, indirect questions and the news

Spanish names its source before the fact: según with a source, al parecer for what the evidence suggests, dicen que and se dice que when nobody is named, según la leyenda for a story told as a legend; por lo visto is the familiar "apparently". The newspaper reports a rumour with the conditional: habría pintado, "is said to have painted". Reporting verbs have their own constructions: asegurar que and declarar que take the indicative, informar de que and advertir de que take de (advertir is "to warn", never "to advertise"), and desmentir que takes the subjunctive. Passing words on moves the tenses back, as in Foundation, lesson 65: a reported fact keeps the indicative (me dijo que había sido), and a reported command takes the subjunctive (me dijo que lo apuntara). An indirect question keeps the accent on the question word and the indicative (no sé quién, ni dónde está); "whether" is si, and the familiar me preguntó que si repeats the que. The news puts the verb first when the event is the news, and a headline drops the auxiliary and the article (Detenido un hombre) or uses the present for a past event (Muere un perro). (lessons 11, 16, 19, 20, 21, 54, 56)

## 7 Se in all its uses

Foundation met se as a reflexive, an impersonal, a passive and an involuntary pronoun (Foundation, lessons 57, 59 and 78). Lesson 65 puts all its jobs in one house. Reflexive and reciprocal: the subject does it to itself, or two people to each other. Se replaces le before lo, la, los and las. Involuntary: the thing happens to a person, who is named by me, te or le. Impersonal: no subject, and a singular verb. Passive: a thing as the subject, and the verb agrees with it. A change that happens by itself: se abrió sola. Completion or intensity: comerse is to eat up, and morirse de miedo is to be scared to death. Some verbs change their meaning with se: quedar and quedarse, llevar and llevarse, echar and echarse a, poner and ponerse, ir and irse; and what is in fashion se lleva. (lessons 9, 19, 24, 26, 30, 33, 65, 70)

## 8 Verbal periphrases and short answers

A periphrasis is a verb placed before an infinitive, a gerund or a participle to add time or manner. Foundation had ir a, tener que, acabar de, llevar, seguir and ir with a gerund (Foundation, lessons 62 and 72). This course adds volver a, "again"; dejar de, "to stop", and no dejar de, "to keep on"; andar with a gerund, going about doing something (familiar); venir a, "roughly"; tener and llevar with a participle that agrees, a finished state or how far something has got; llevar with a period and a gerund; ponerse a and echarse a, to start, echarse a for a sudden start; soler and acostumbrar a for a habit; quedar por and estar por, still to be done; estar a punto de, about to. A short answer does not repeat the clause: creo que sí, creo que no, eso espero; lo stands for an adjective already said (lo parece, no lo son); también answers a positive and tampoco a negative. ## 9 Relatives, cuyo and el cual

Foundation taught que, quien, el que, lo que, el cual, lo cual and cuyo (Foundation, lessons 64, 66 and 80). English can leave the preposition at the end of the clause; Spanish puts it before the relative, with the article: en la que, con el que, bajo el cual. After a preposition quien is used for a person (con quien), el cual is formal and el que is the everyday form. Lo cual and lo que point back to a whole statement. Cuyo agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner: cuya obra, cuyas paredes, cuyo nombre. Commas around the clause add information about something already named; without commas the clause picks out a group (los alumnos que estaban cansados se fueron: only the tired ones left). El que, la de and el del stand for a noun already said or easy to guess: el que invita, la de Argentina, el del quinto. (lessons 3, 4, 17, 23, 24, 28, 43, 51, 52, 56)

## 10 Sino, pero and the connectors of an argument

Pero adds a contrast, and both parts stand; sino corrects a negative and replaces the first part (no es pequeño, sino práctico), and sino que does the same with a verb. No solo ... sino también is "not only ... but also". In a written argument, sin embargo and the more formal no obstante go against what was expected; en cambio and por el contrario set one thing against another; a diferencia de takes a noun, mientras que a clause. Por consiguiente and así pues draw the result, es decir restates, en primer lugar and por un lado ... por otro put the points in order, and en definitiva and a fin de cuentas close. For a cause, porque joins a reason to a clause, and como gives the reason first and must open the sentence; ya que is a reason the listener already knows, puesto que and dado que are formal, and written pues gives a reason after the main clause. Most of these can open the sentence, with a comma after them; en cambio usually sits between commas after the first word or phrase. (lessons 6, 12, 13, 14, 54, 59, 63, 64, 68, 70)

|---|---|---|

## 11 The small words of conversation

Spoken Spanish sets its tone with small words, and none of them is translated word for word: ask who is speaking, to whom and in what mood. Que at the start of a reply presses a point (¡que no!) or gives the reason asked for (que ya está pagado, "the thing is, it's paid"). Ni hablar refuses, qué va denies, venga and vale agree, anda pushes someone to get on with it, hombre and mujer are said between friends, and pues starts a reply. ## 12 Register: tú, usted and the general you

In Spain tú is the form for most adults, among friends, family, colleagues and young people; usted is kept for older people, formal service and distance (Foundation, lessons 2, 13 and 93). Moving from one to the other is a small social event: hablar de usted or tratar de usted is to use usted with someone, and tutear is to use tú. The choice shows in the verb, the pronoun and the possessive: usted takes the third person, le and su, so perdone against perdona and le ayudo against te ayudo, and a speaker who slips back is noticed. The general "you" is tú, as in English (entras, te acercas y pides), and uno with a verb in the third person is "one". A waiter is called with ¡Oiga!, the usted form, or ¡Perdona!, the tú form, never with a wave of the hand. In Madrid you will hear le for a man as the direct object (le vieron), which the Academy accepts; la for "to her" is heard too, but the standard is le (le dije). Register also chooses the words: the instancia and the town hall's reply in section 13, the news in section 6, the familiar pasar de, ¡qué guay! or tirar los tejos in the notes. ## 13 Formal letters and administrative style

A request to the town hall, the instancia, has a fixed order: the name, age, occupation and address of the person who signs; comparece and EXPONE, then each fact opening with Que; SOLICITA: que with the present subjunctive; Sírvase with an infinitive, the formal "please"; Ruego que with the subjunctive; Sin otro particular, le saluda atentamente, and the signature. The signer calls herself la abajo firmante (a man is el abajo firmante). Formal links: conforme a lo dispuesto, en relación con, en virtud de, en cuanto a. The reply opens with participles and gerunds (Visto el expediente, Considerando que), then Consta en, Se desestima and asimismo, the commands with se Procédase and Notifíquese, and cabe recurso. Plain Spanish says the same with está en, se rechaza, también and sobre. The future subjunctive in -re (infringiere, hubiere, fuere) survives only in laws and set phrases; ordinary Spanish uses the present subjunctive. A letter to a friend or a reader opens with Querido or Querida and a colon, and closes with Un fuerte abrazo, Con cariño or, a little more formally, Un cordial saludo. (lessons 47, 48, 49, 69)

## 14 The position of the adjective, superlatives and exclamations

An adjective after the noun says what kind of thing it is; before the noun it often gives a judgement, and a few change their meaning. Gran before a singular noun is "great", grande after it is "big". Propio before the noun is "own"; mismo before it is "same" and after it "itself"; único before it is "only" and after it "unique"; antiguo before it is "former" and after it "old". The ending -ísimo makes the absolute superlative (Foundation, lesson 47), with the spelling changes that keep the sound: rico, riquísimo; tierno, tiernísimo. Exclamations: ¡qué + noun + tan + adjective!, ¡cuántos + noun! (cuánto agrees with the noun), and lo + adjective + que, "how", where the adjective agrees (lo bonita que era). Colour adjectives in -izo, -áceo and -oso mean "tending to" the colour: rojizo, grisáceo, verdoso; tirar a says the same with a verb. (lessons 23, 52, 53, 56, 67, 70)

## 15 Ser and estar: the finer points

The rule of Foundation holds (Foundation, lessons 2 to 4, 60 and 64), and this course refines it. Where a thing is takes estar (está situada sobre una colina, está en Londres), but where an event takes place takes ser (la Feria de Abril es en Sevilla). Ser with a participle is the act, often with a date and with the doer after por; estar with a participle is the state that results: fueron pintados en 1814, but cuyas paredes estaban pintadas. Ser in the past says what a thing was, a role that has ended, and ser in the present what it is now. How someone feels today takes estar, so estás contento, never eres contento; estar is also the state already reached (ya estaba sentado, ya estaba dormido). A participle phrase describes without a verb, and agrees with its noun: situada a orillas del Guadalquivir. (lessons 4, 7, 8, 16, 23, 24, 28, 29, 32, 41, 55)

## 16 Por and para: the finer points

Foundation set out the main uses (Foundation, lesson 84). Para with an infinitive is for the same subject, para que with the subjunctive for another one: para comprobar, but para que el oído se acostumbre. ## 17 False friends, calques, double senses and the norm

Some Spanish words look like English ones and mean something else. Actualmente is "currently", and "actually" is en realidad; eventualmente is "possibly, at some point", and "eventually" is al final; extrañar in Spain is "to surprise", and "to miss" is echar de menos; realizar is "to carry out", and "to realise" is darse cuenta; advertir is "to warn"; éxito is "success"; bastante is "quite" as well as "enough"; suspender is to fail an exam, and aprobar to pass it. Calques copy an English pattern: aplicar para un trabajo for solicitar un puesto, hacer sentido for tener sentido, es por eso que for por eso, atender for asistir a, introducir for presentar, soportar for apoyar, llamar para atrás for devolver la llamada. One form can carry two senses (cola is a tail and a queue, banco a bench and a bank), and the article can change the word (la frente, the forehead; el frente, the front). The norm also corrects native speakers: pensar que without de, alegrarse de que with it, impersonal haber always in the singular (había muchas personas), a ver si in two words, and the accent that tells words apart: tú and tu, él and el, sí and si, por qué, porqué and porque. ## 18 Spanish across the map

The Spanish of this course is that of Spain, but five portraits listen to other varieties, and each word is named with its place and its Spanish equivalent. The Canary Islands: seseo, ustedes for the plural "you", guagua and papa. ## 19 Verse: syllables, sinalefa and assonance

Lorca's romances and the sobremesa of lesson 34 use the romance, the old Spanish ballad: lines of eight syllables, with assonance on the even lines. Count the syllables as they are spoken. Sinalefa: a vowel at the end of a word joins the vowel at the start of the next into one syllable, and a silent h does not stop it (se_a-ca-bó; se_ha). A diphthong (ie, ue, ua, ui) is one syllable, and an accent on the weak vowel splits it (frí-o, tí-a). The last word adjusts the count: a line ending in a word stressed on the next-to-last syllable counts as written; one stressed on the last counts one more; one ending in an esdrújula counts one less, so la tía habla de política has nine syllables as written and counts eight. Assonance repeats only the vowels from the last stressed one: levanta, montaña, tarta and calla all end in á-a; a full rhyme repeats the consonants too. (lessons 32, 34, 35, 59)
