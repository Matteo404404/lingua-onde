== 1 A1
numeral: Lección primera
numeral.pron.en: lék*cion* pri*mé*ra
title: En el bar
title.en: At the bar

dialogue:
- ¡Buenos días!
  en: Good morning! (Good days!)
  pron.en: ¡*boué*noss *di*ass!
- Buenos días. ¿Qué le pongo?
  en: Good morning. What can I get you? (What [for] you do I put?)
  pron.en: *boué*noss *di*ass. ¿ké lé *pon*go?
- Un café con leche, por favor.
  en: A white coffee, please. (A coffee with milk, for favour.)
  pron.en: oun ca*fé* con *lé*tché, por fa*bor*.
- ¿Algo más?
  en: Anything else? (Something more?)
  pron.en: ¿*al*go mass?
- Sí, una tostada con tomate.
  en: Yes, a [slice of] toast with tomato.
  pron.en: si, *ou*na toss*ta*da con to*ma*té.
- Aquí tiene.
  en: Here you are. (Here [you] have.)
  pron.en: a*ki* *tié*né.
- Muchas gracias.
  en: Thank you very much. (Many thanks.)
  pron.en: *mou*tchass *gra*ciass.

sounds.en:
1. Under each sentence you find the figured pronunciation: read it as if it were English, with the conventions of the pronunciation guide. The syllable that carries the stress is in bold, in every word of two or more syllables; words of one syllable have none. Every e is written é, so that you never leave it silent, and u is written ou, as in soup: *boué*noss *di*ass.
2. Spanish vowels are short and clear, and they never change: an unstressed vowel keeps its full sound and is never swallowed into the "uh" of English. Leche is *lé*tché and tomate is to*ma*té, with the last é sounded. An s is always the s of see, never the z of rose, so the figured line writes ss inside a word: *mou*tchass.
3. In Madrid a c before e or i is said with the tongue between the teeth, like the th of think: gracias is *gra*ciass. The v of favor is a b (fa*bor*), and the r is a single quick tap of the tongue, like the tt of "better" said fast.

notes.en:
1. Buenos días: good morning, literally "good days", in the plural. It is said until lunch, which in Spain is at about two o'clock, so the Spanish morning is a long one. The afternoon greeting comes later in the course.
2. ¿Qué le pongo?: what the barman says to every customer, literally "what [for] you do I put?". Notice the upside-down question mark: Spanish opens every question with ¿ and every exclamation with ¡, so that you know from the first word how the sentence will sound.
3. Un café con leche: every Spanish noun is either masculine or feminine, and "a" is un before a masculine noun and una before a feminine one: un café, una tostada. Most nouns ending in -o are masculine and most ending in -a feminine, but café shows there are others: learn each noun with its un or una. A café con leche is half coffee, half hot milk: the Spanish white coffee.
4. Por favor: please, literally "for favour". It can go at the end of the sentence, as here, or at the beginning.
5. ¿Algo más?: anything else? Algo means "something" or "anything", and más means "more". Más carries a written accent, which also tells you where the stress falls when a word has more than one syllable.
6. Aquí tiene: here you are, literally "here [you] have". The barman does not say "you": the ending of the verb is enough, and Spanish usually leaves the person out. We will come back to this in the next lesson. Aquí, here, is stressed on its last syllable, as its written accent shows: a*ki*.
7. Muchas gracias: thank you very much, literally "many thanks". Mucho, "much, many", agrees with its noun: gracias is feminine and plural, so it becomes muchas.

ex1:
- ¡Buenos días!
  en: Good morning!
- Un café, por favor.
  en: A coffee, please.
- ¿Algo más?
  en: Anything else?
- Una tostada con tomate.
  en: Toast with tomato.
- Aquí tiene.
  en: Here you are.

ex2:
- [Buenos] [días].
  en: Good morning.
- Un café [con] [leche], por favor.
  en: A coffee with milk, please.
- ¿[Algo] [más]?
  en: Anything else?
- [Muchas] [gracias].
  en: Thank you very much.

culture.en:
Many Spaniards have breakfast at the bar, standing at the counter on the way to work or in a break in the middle of the morning. The classic order is the one in the dialogue: a café con leche and a tostada, a slice of toasted bread with grated tomato and olive oil, or with butter and jam. A small black coffee is un café solo, and a cortado is a black coffee "cut" with a dash of milk. Nobody minds a short order: two or three words and por favor are polite enough, and you usually pay at the end, at the counter. To ask how much it is, say: ¿Cuánto es?

== 2 A1
numeral: Lección segunda
numeral.pron.en: lék*cion* sé*goun*da
title: ¿Cómo te llamas?
title.en: What's your name?

dialogue:
- Hola. ¿Cómo te llamas?
  en: Hello. What's your name? (How yourself do [you] call?)
  pron.en: *o*la. ¿*co*mo té *ya*mass?
- Me llamo Fiona. ¿Y tú?
  en: My name is Fiona. (Myself [I] call Fiona.) And you?
  pron.en: mé *ya*mo *fio*na. ¿i tou?
- Yo soy Javier. ¿Eres inglesa?
  en: I'm Javier. Are [you] English?
  pron.en: yo soï Ha*biér*. ¿*é*réss in*glé*ssa?
- No, soy escocesa. De Edimburgo.
  en: No, I'm Scottish. From Edinburgh.
  pron.en: no, soï éssco*cé*ssa. dé édim*bour*go.
- Yo soy de aquí, de Madrid.
  en: I'm from here, from Madrid.
  pron.en: yo soï dé a*ki*, dé ma*drid*.
- Encantada.
  en: Pleased to meet you. (Delighted, said by a woman.)
  pron.en: éncan*ta*da.
- Encantado.
  en: Pleased to meet you. (Delighted, said by a man.)
  pron.en: éncan*ta*do.

sounds.en:
1. The j of Javier is a sound English does not have: a rough breath at the back of the throat, like the ch of Scottish loch. The figured line writes it H: Ha*biér*. Notice the v too, said b, as in lesson 1.
2. The h of Spanish is always silent: hola is *o*la. The double l is said like the y of yes: llamas is *ya*mass, llamo *ya*mo.
3. Remember the stress rule. Without a written accent, a word ending in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the next-to-last syllable: éssco*cé*ssa, *ya*mo. A word ending in any other consonant is stressed on the last: ma*drid*. A written accent breaks the rule and shows where the stress goes: *co*mo.

notes.en:
1. ¿Cómo te llamas?: what's your name? Literally "how do you call yourself?". The answer is me llamo, "I call myself": me llamo Fiona, my name is Fiona. Cómo, how, has a written accent in a question.
2. ¿Y tú?: and you? Y means "and", and tú is the "you" for one person you talk to as a friend: a young person, a colleague, a member of your family. At a language exchange everybody says tú. Strangers in a shop or older people are usually spoken to with usted, as the barman did in lesson 1. Tú has a written accent; tu without one means "your".
3. Yo soy Javier: I am Javier. Soy, "I am", and eres, "you are", come from ser, "to be". Spanish does not need the yo: the ending of soy already says "I". Javier uses it because he is setting himself against Fiona, "and me, I'm Javier"; when she answers she simply says soy escocesa. English cannot leave out its "I"; Spanish leaves it out most of the time.
4. ¿Eres inglesa?: are you English? A question needs no change of order in Spanish: eres inglesa, "you are English", becomes a question with the question marks and the voice rising at the end.
5. Inglesa, escocesa: words of nationality agree with the person: a man is inglés or escocés, a woman inglesa or escocesa. In Spanish they take no capital letter. The written accent of inglés goes away in inglesa: the word now ends in a vowel, so the stress falls on the next-to-last syllable by the rule, in*glé*ssa.
6. De Edimburgo, de Madrid: de means "from" (and also "of"). Soy de Madrid: I'm from Madrid.
7. Encantada, encantado: pleased to meet you, literally "delighted". Like the words of nationality, it agrees with the person who says it: Fiona says encantada, Javier encantado.

ex1:
- Hola, me llamo Fiona.
  en: Hello, my name is Fiona.
- ¿Cómo te llamas?
  en: What's your name?
- Soy escocesa.
  en: I'm Scottish. (a woman speaking)
- ¿Eres de Madrid?
  en: Are you from Madrid?
- Encantado.
  en: Pleased to meet you. (a man speaking)

ex2:
- ¿[Cómo] te [llamas]?
  en: What's your name?
- [Me] [llamo] Javier.
  en: My name is Javier.
- ¿[Eres] inglesa?
  en: Are you English?
- Soy [de] Madrid.
  en: I'm from Madrid.

culture.en:
Spaniards move to first names and to tú very quickly: two people introduced at a party or a bar say tú from the first sentence, as Fiona and Javier do. When they are introduced, women, and a man and a woman, greet each other with dos besos, two light kisses, one on each cheek, even at a first meeting; two men usually shake hands. In Madrid you will also find many intercambios, evenings in a bar where Spaniards and foreigners meet to swap languages: half an hour in English, half an hour in Spanish. They are the best place to say, for the first time and to a real person: Encantado, or Encantada.
