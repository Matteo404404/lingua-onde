# Mastery writer: what every week must follow

Read first, in this order:
1. work/BRIEF.md: section "Mastery" (and "Checks", with mastery in place of
   foundation); the Foundation and Perfectionnement sections give the
   learner's background and the house style.
2. METHOD.md section 3, content/FORMAT.md (`tasks:`, `task.it:`, `rubric.it:`,
   `refs:`).
3. The plan: work/en/mastery.md (rules at the top, then your week's table).
4. The approved lessons of this course written so far (work/en/mastery/), all
   of the previous week. Before Mastery 8 exists, read Perfectionnement
   lessons 59, 64, 65, 66 and 70 (work/en/perfectionnement/) for the note
   style, the Italian, the tone of the jokes and a revision lesson.
5. Perfectionnement and Foundation are known to the learner:
   work/en/perfectionnement/*.txt, work/en/foundation/*.txt.

Files: work/en/mastery/NNN.txt, one lesson each, starting:

    course: en
    stage: mastery
    bases: it

    == N C1      (C2 from lesson 26)
    numeral: Lesson Eight
    title: ...
    title.it: ...
    kind: ...    (only `kind: revision` on revision lessons)

Shape (BRIEF "Mastery" is the authority):
- `dialogue:` one item per numbered sentence of the text: 25–40 items,
  350–700 words, of the kind and with the ending the plan gives. Each item
  has `it:` with a free, natural Italian translation (idioms first literally
  in brackets only where it helps). No pron lines, no sounds, no culture.
- `notes.it:` 10–18 notes on register and its shifts, what is meant but not
  said, irony, cultural and historical references, idioms and collocations,
  the precise word against its near-synonyms, rhetoric; contrasts with
  Italian. Each note starts on its own line ("1. ...") with the words of the
  text, exactly as the dialogue has them.
- `ex1:` five demanding sentences or short passages to translate, where the
  nuance matters, each with `it:`. `ex2:` five collocations, idioms or
  connectors in context, one or two words per [blank], with `it:`; each blank
  has only one right answer given the Italian.
- `tasks:` the plan's one or two tasks. Each item: the model answer as the
  item's text (English, of the length the task asks), `task.it:` the
  instruction in Italian, `rubric.it:` four or five self-check points
  separated by `;` (meaning; register; structure; idiom; effect, adapted).
- Revision lessons (7, 14 ... 98): `kind: revision`; `dialogue:` the revision
  text, 20–30 items, each with `refs:` to the week's lessons its words or
  devices come from; `notes.it:` a few lines, then four to six numbered
  sections that put the week's focus in order, with tables (`| ... |` rows)
  and examples quoted from the week's texts; no ex1/ex2; one task.
- `aside.it:` only where the plan says.

Rules:
- Everything original. Do not open the reference course books (kept outside the repository). Literary pieces are "in the
  manner of" an era or genre, never imitating a living author's text; quote
  only titles or a few words of public-domain works. Real places, people,
  history: only well-established facts; when unsure, leave it out.
- Natural British English of the register the plan asks for, and natural
  Italian. Every English sentence must make sense in context as a native
  reader would read it; linkers must be logical.
- Italian text names the earlier courses "Fondamenti" and "Perfezionamento",
  never "Foundation" or "Perfectionnement"; this course is "Padronanza".
- References: "(lezione N)" or "(lezione N, nota M)" is this course.
  "(Perfezionamento, lezione N, nota M)" and "(Fondamenti, lezione N)" name
  the earlier courses; one course per bracket. Every reference must point to
  a note that really treats that word or device: open it and check.
- A note explains only what is in its own sentence, and never claims
  something about another text without rereading it. When a note says who
  does what in the text, check it against the text.
- Grammar and usage claims exact: no "always/never" where usage varies; say
  what is usual and what is also heard. "In speech one would say ..." must be
  grammatical.
- Explain idioms in their real sense first. Irony and implicature: say what
  is said, what is meant, and how the reader knows.
- Exercises restate the lesson's facts exactly; each ex1 item a natural,
  self-contained statement.
- Stress, where a note needs it, with the signs of
  content/en/pronunciation.md only (stressed syllable between asterisks,
  ‣ long vowel, ë, θ, dh, sc/sc', c/c', gi/g', y; è only for the vowel of
  cat; no dj, dg or ò; no asterisks on one-syllable words). Rarely needed.
- Italian: one form of address per speaker through a lesson; the course
  speaks to the learner with tu; polite lei, le, la in lowercase; workers
  and strangers address adults they do not know with lei.
- Keep the British word the text uses (queue, flat, pavement).
- Model answers in tasks are good, natural English of the asked length and
  register, not a copy of the text, and they meet their own rubric (if the
  rubric asks for contractions, the model has them).

Rules added after week 1:
- Notes are dense, not padded. Use "what is said / what is meant / how the
  reader knows" only where implicature is the point of the note, not in
  every note. No disclaimers of the kind "il testo non dice che..." unless a
  reader would really misread the sentence.
- The Italian of ex2 never contains the English answer or any English word
  ("È stato rilevato che...", not "...nella formula it has been noted...").
  No English words in Italian notes outside the quoted text (no stray
  "because", no English sentence).
- References in brackets only: "(Fondamenti, lezione 3, nota 2)", never
  "dai Fondamenti (lezione 3, nota 2)".
- The story must be plausible: a twist needs a reason the reader accepts
  (a friend who runs the hotel took it over recently and said so in an
  unread email, not an "oldest friend" whose job the narrator never knew).
  Places and means of transport stay consistent (bus there, bus back).
- Italian: "ed è", "uno sfogo", "uno scostamento"; ex1/ex2 translations keep
  the form of address the dialogue uses (an interviewer gives lei).
- A table's columns hold what their headers say: check every row.

Rules added after week 2:
- Notes never speak in the writer's own voice ("non fisso un anno...") and
  never mix an English clause into the Italian ("who is herself ahead of...").
- A note is at most about 120 words; a note that needs more is two notes.
  Do not re-tell the plot in the last note: say what the ending does, briefly.
- Everyday British facts must hold: public libraries are free (a "ticket" is
  renewed, not paid for); trains, routes and prices plausible.
- A revision text introduces nothing the week did not have (no ferry from
  nowhere).
- ex2 translations are plain Italian: no hints about English spelling and
  no glosses on the answer.

Rules added after week 5:
- Any count the text states (eight opinions, fifteen words) must be true:
  count before you write it, and recount it in the revision.
- A reference names the note that really says the thing; if no earlier note
  says it, give no reference.
- ex2 Italian is a translation of the sentence, never a riddle ("quella
  parola", "l'altro verbo", "nella forma più formale").
- No line contradicts another (have a bath is British, so the British do
  not take a bath a few lines later).

Rules added after week 6:
- A legal or official document must fit the law on its own date (for
  example: deposits are capped at five weeks' rent; fixed-term ASTs end with
  the Renters' Rights Act from May 2026). If unsure, date it earlier and say
  so in a note.
- One name, one person across the course week: no two Leahs.
- A revision never gives a character an act that was someone else's.

Rules added after week 7:
- Invented books and studies use invented publishers and bodies, never a
  real imprint (no Clarendon Press for a book that does not exist).
- Italian renders English exactly: modest is "modesta", not "modestissima";
  no calques like "declinare di" or "borsa slogan".
- Notes are Italian with English words quoted, never English labels such as
  "triad" or "Tonality".

Rules added after week 8:
- Never copy brief instructions into lesson text (no "mai Foundation nelle
  note").
- A dialect feature must be the real one: Indian "only" and "itself" are
  emphasis ("today itself"), not standard uses; Irish "bold" is mainly
  "naughty". If unsure of a feature, leave it out.
- Revisions reuse the week's people with their own names and places; no new
  "Aunty Pat" for an unnamed grandmother.
- Days stay consistent inside a lesson (a weekend guest does not learn
  something "by Friday").

Rules added after week 9:
- No premise or structure of a famous sketch, song, poem or book, even
  "original" in wording (no shop without its product with the customer
  listing items one by one). Invent the situation.
- No checker's or writer's memo left in the text ("verificare: ...").
- Invented bodies never sit inside real institutions (no "Home Office
  bookshop" for an invented department).

Rules added after week 10:
- Copyright rules stay in the brief, never in the lesson: no "not a living
  poet's protected lines", "public-domain tradition", "not a lift".
- Technical claims about sound must be true: a slant rhyme shares a vowel
  or a final consonant (route / boots), an assonance is not a full rhyme
  (late / grey, not grey / day). Facts about real poems too (L'infinito is
  unrhymed).
- Counts of stanzas, lines and feet match the text.

Rules added after week 11:
- Originality notes ("invented examples only", "no raid on living papers",
  "not lifting a famous sketch") belong to you, not to the lesson text.
- A surname belongs to one family in a week: no June Harwick beside Tom
  Harwick "as a different person".
- Model answers do what their task asks (if the task wants the missing
  evidence for each fallacy, the model gives it) and add no facts the
  rubric forbids.

Rules added after week 12:
- Sports facts must add up: the side that wins "by two wickets" is the side
  that was batting with two wickets in hand.
- A revision repeats what happened (Harwick stopped a ball; he did not
  catch one).
- ex2 Italian never replaces the answer with a description ("quella formula
  di correzione a due parole"): translate the sentence.

Rules added after week 13:
- British characters speak British English: full stop, not period; check
  every AmE word in a British mouth.
- "Three words" means three words; a list of phrases is not three words.
- Revision scenes stay plausible in their place (no retirement speech about
  a fern in a hospital; "station" means the nurses' station only if it says
  so).

Rules added after week 14:
- People of another century stay in their century: a revision never puts
  the writers of a 1600 letter at a modern wedding; modern people may quote
  the letter. No anachronism inside a period piece (no postman in 1600).
- Dates of words must hold: "democracy" is not a seventeenth-century
  scientific loan; check when a word entered English before using it as an
  example.
- A model answer that the rubric wants made with different tools uses
  different tools (three compounds are not "compound, blend, conversion").
- Grammar labels are exact: "if thou approve" is a subjunctive, not an -est
  form.

Checks before you finish:
- `target/release/marea --check work/en/mastery/NNN.txt` prints ok
  with no problem and no `gap:` line, for each file.
- Count items and words of each text against the plan's ranges.
- Reread every lesson: the ending and the joke work and the notes explain
  them correctly; one answer per blank; no doubtful fact; every reference
  verified by opening the cited text.
- Touch nothing but your own lesson files. No git, no cargo.

Report briefly: per lesson items, words, notes, tasks; checker output; facts
you were unsure of; any choice the plan did not settle.
