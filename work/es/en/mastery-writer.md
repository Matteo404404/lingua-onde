## Shape (BRIEF "Mastery" is the authority)

- `dialogue:` one item per numbered sentence of the text: 25–40 items,
  350–700 words, of the kind and with the ending the plan gives. Each item
  has `en:` with a free, natural British English translation; for an idiom,
  the English meaning first, then the literal sense in brackets, only where
  it helps: "he pulled my leg (he took my hair)". No [square brackets] at this
  level: they belong to Foundation.
- `notes.en:` 10–18 notes, in the order of the text, on register and its
  shifts, what is meant but not said, irony, cultural and historical
  references, idioms and collocations, the precise word against its
  near-synonyms, rhetoric; contrasts with English. Each note starts on its
  own line ("1. ...") with the words of the text, exactly as the dialogue
  has them. A note is at most about 120 words; a note that needs more is two
  notes. Do not re-tell the plot in the last note: say what the ending does,
  briefly.
- `ex1:` five demanding sentences or short passages to translate, where the
  nuance matters, each with `en:`; new sentences, never copies of text
  lines. From lesson 50 the learner rebuilds them from the English, so the
  English must point to one natural Spanish sentence: where tú, usted,
  vosotros or ustedes matters, the English settles it with context (a name
  with señor or señora, "you two", "kids", "sir").
- `ex2:` five collocations, idioms or connectors in context, one or two
  words per [blank], with `en:` the plain English sentence (never a hint, a
  gloss or a riddle such as "that word", "in the more formal form"). Each
  blank has only one right answer given the English. Never blank a word the
  English cannot tell from a near-synonym or a second correct form: a verb
  whose tú or usted form the English leaves open; the imperfect subjunctive
  (hablara and hablase are both right); lo and le for a male person (both
  accepted); este, ese, aquel; pero and sino unless the negation settles it;
  porque, ya que, puesto que; aunque, a pesar de que; sin embargo, no
  obstante; así que, por lo tanto, por eso; quizá, quizás, tal vez; morir,
  fallecer; the perfect and the preterite where no time word decides.
- `tasks:` the plan's one or two tasks. Each item: the model answer as the
  item's text (Spanish, of the length the task asks), `task.en:` the
  instruction in English, `rubric.en:` four or five self-check points
  separated by `;` (meaning; register; structure; idiom; effect, adapted).
- Revision lessons (7, 14 ... 98): `kind: revision`; `dialogue:` the revision
  text, 20–30 items, each with `refs:` to the week's lessons its words or
  devices come from; `notes.en:` a few lines, then four to six numbered
  sections that put the week's focus in order, with tables (`| ... |` rows)
  and examples quoted from the week's texts; no ex1/ex2; one task.
- `aside.en:` only where the plan says (lesson 49).
- Free text is never hard-wrapped: each note, each line of a revision
  introduction and the aside are one line each; only table rows take one
  line per row.

## Rules

- Everything original. Do not open the reference course books (kept outside
  the repository). Literary pieces are "in the manner of" an era or genre,
  never imitating a living author's text; quote only titles or a few words
  of public-domain works (Cervantes, Bécquer, Darío, the Cantar de mio Cid);
  modern authors by name and title only. No premise or structure of a famous
  sketch, song, poem or book (Larra's "Vuelva usted mañana" is named, never
  retold). Invented books, studies, papers, clubs, restaurants and companies
  use invented names, never a real publisher, newspaper, TV or radio
  programme or institution, and never sit inside a real one.
- Real places, people, history: only well-established facts; when unsure,
  leave it out. Legends and stories told about people are told as such
  ("según se cuenta", "cuenta la leyenda"). Facts in the plan were checked:
  keep them. If you change or drop one, say so in the report with the
  reason; never replace a true fact with a guess, and never add a date,
  number or name you are not sure of. Things that may have changed recently
  (a law, a rule of the RAE, an institution's name, where something is kept)
  are written so they stay true: "tradicionalmente", "desde hace años", or
  left out. Use the current name of a museum or institution. History and
  status facts need their exact stages.
- Legal and official documents fit Spanish practice; if unsure of a rule,
  keep the document generic and lawful (an association's own statutes, not
  a law). A figure is right in its order of magnitude or left out.
- The Spanish of Spain as Madrid speaks and writes it today, spelled by the
  RAE: ¿ and ¡ at the start, every written accent (tú/tu, él/el, mí/mi,
  sí/si, sé/se, dé/de, más/mas, aún/aun, qué and cómo in indirect
  questions), solo and the demonstratives without accent, guion, years
  without a point (1492), « » for quoted speech, lower case for days,
  months, languages and nationalities, a capital only on the first word of a
  title (Cien años de soledad). Spain's words and forms in the text (coche,
  ordenador, móvil, zumo, piso, conducir, coger, vale, vosotros, the perfect
  for today, the preterite for a finished time), of the register the plan
  asks for. Natural Spanish, nothing a Madrileño would find odd. Every
  Spanish sentence must make sense in context as a native reader would read
  it; linkers must be logical ("pero" joins two things that really
  contrast).
- Standard grammar in the narration and in every speaker unless the plan
  makes a feature the point (the grandmother's laísmo in lesson 55): lo and
  la for the direct object (le for a male person is accepted; keep one
  choice through the lesson), no laísmo or loísmo, no dequeísmo (pienso que,
  me alegro de que), impersonal haber in the singular (había muchas
  personas), the vosotros imperative in -d (venid, mirad) and -os with the
  pronoun (sentaos, callaos), never the infinitive as an imperative, no
  gerund for a later event (never "se cayó, rompiéndose la pierna" for "y se
  rompió").
- Other varieties only in the lessons the plan sets there or about them (50
  to 56, 81, 95, 96, and notes that name a region or country): the narrator
  writes the Spanish of Spain unless the plan says a character speaks
  another variety; each regional word or form is named as such in the note
  ("in Mexico ...", "in the Río de la Plata ...", "in the Canary Islands
  ...") with the Madrid equivalent. A word of one country or region is
  presented as that, never as "Latin American" or "Andalusian" in general,
  and never as standard. Real features only; if unsure of one, leave it out.
  Write a variety's sounds in normal spelling in the Spanish line (Madrid,
  not Madrit) unless the plan quotes a spelling, and describe the sound in
  the note.
- Address in Spanish: tú among friends, family, colleagues, young people,
  and by default in Spain today; usted to strangers who are older, officials,
  customers in formal settings, and wherever the plan sets it; vosotros for
  "you all" in Spain, ustedes where the plan sets the scene in the Canaries,
  western Andalusia or Latin America. When the plan is about the switch, the
  switch is the point and must be visible. One form per speaker through the
  lesson, also in ex1, ex2 and tasks; the note says which form a speaker
  uses where English cannot show it.
- The Spanish lines never contain English words, not even in quotes ("un
  billete es a ticket" is wrong): explain in Spanish inside the text, and put
  the English only in the `en:` line and the notes. Loanwords that are
  standard Spanish of Spain (fútbol, wifi, máster, OK, sándwich, el chat) are
  Spanish words. The anglicisms that are the point of lessons 81 and 96
  (bro, random, cringe, running, brunch, and the Spanglish of the plan) are
  allowed there, and quoted Catalan, Galician or Basque words in lesson 55.
- English: natural British English, plain and warm, you to the learner
  ("notice", "remember"). British spelling and words (colour, realise, flat,
  mobile, lift, underground, holiday, autumn, pavement, motorway, petrol,
  queue, chemist's, post, bin, timetable, secondary school). English prose
  stays English: quote a Spanish word as an example, never use it as an
  English word ("she took the bus", not "she took the guagua"), except the
  established English loans (tapas, siesta, fiesta, paella, patio, plaza).
  Notes never contain a Spanish sentence that explains ("léelo en voz alta"
  is wrong). The English renders the Spanish exactly, without additions and
  without calques. Grammar labels are exact: present perfect, preterite,
  imperfect, pluperfect, conditional, present and imperfect subjunctive,
  gerund, past participle, impersonal se.
- Names: "Spain", "Spanish" (castellano is named where the note is about the
  name), "Latin America"; English names of places where they are current
  (Seville, Andalusia, Catalonia, Castile, Navarre, Aragon, the Basque
  Country, the Canary Islands, the Balearic Islands, Mexico City), Spanish
  names otherwise (Cádiz, Córdoba, Zaragoza, A Coruña, Vigo, Quito).
- In English text the earlier courses are "Foundation" and
  "Perfectionnement"; this course is "Mastery".
- References: "(lesson N)" or "(lesson N, note M)" is this course;
  "(Perfectionnement, lesson N, note M)" and "(Foundation, lesson N)" name
  the earlier courses; one course per bracket, references in brackets only
  ("(Foundation, lesson 3)", never "from Foundation (lesson 3)"). N is the
  lesson whose DIALOGUE first has that word, found with
  `python3 -I work/tools/first_seen.py work/es/en/foundation 'regex'` (or
  .../perfectionnement, .../mastery); for a grammar point, the lesson whose
  note explains it (the plans name it): open the lesson and check the note
  says it. Every "sentence N", "note N" and "lesson N, note M" must point at
  the right sentence or note: open it and count. If no earlier note says it,
  give no reference. Every reference fits the course order.
- No recurring characters: a name used in an earlier lesson is another
  person; never write "you met him in lesson N" about a character. One
  name, one person, and a surname belongs to one family, within a week.
  Spanish people have two surnames (Foundation 10); use them where a
  document would.
- Dialogues hold together line by line: each line answers the one before
  it, nobody asks what they have just said or been told, nobody repeats a
  question already answered, and a speaker knows only what their role knows.
  Read the dialogue aloud as two people before you write the notes. One
  speaker per line; speakers alternate unless a line says otherwise.
- A story keeps its own facts straight from line to line: the same person
  does the same thing; who pays whom, who knows what, what is already true at
  the start (a phone on silent cannot ring); places, days, times, clocks,
  amounts and means of transport stay put and add up. Any count the text
  states (eight opinions, three stanzas) is true: count before you write it.
  The twist must be plausible and follow from the whole text, the closing
  line pays off the premise, and the note explains the joke the text really
  tells.
- Tenses: a history text keeps the past (preterite for events, imperfect
  for background) and the present only for what is still true today; no
  slip into the historical present halfway. A story told in the present
  (lesson 22) stays in it. People of another century stay in their century,
  with nothing anachronistic inside a period piece; modern people may quote
  them.
- Idioms and fixed phrases: only ones you are sure exist in the Spanish of
  Spain, in their real shape (estar en las nubes, no tener pelos en la
  lengua, tomar el pelo a alguien), used in their real sense; explain the
  real sense first (estar sin blanca = to be broke), then the literal image,
  and give a word's origin only if it is right. Reflexive idioms keep their
  pronoun (no te comas la cabeza). If the scene does not fit the sense,
  change the scene; if you are not sure a phrase is said that way, write a
  plainer sentence.
- Grammar and usage claims exact and true in general: no "always", "never"
  where usage varies; say what is usual and what is also heard; if a rule
  has exceptions or you are not sure, describe only the example in the text.
  Keep the truths Foundation taught (ser and estar; the perfect for a time
  not over and the preterite for one that is, in Spain; the imperfect for
  scene and habit; gustar turned round; the personal a; pronoun order and
  se lo; si + present, never si + future; the subjunctive after wishes,
  doubt, purpose and the future with cuando). Check irregular forms you are
  not sure of (satisfacer → satisfizo, caber → cupo, prever → previó).
- Irony and implicature: say what is said, what is meant, and how the reader
  knows, only where implicature is the point of the note, not in every note.
  No disclaimers of the kind "the text does not say that ..." unless a
  reader would really misread the sentence.
- Verse (lessons 59, 64–70 and any poem in a task): every line a full
  Spanish sentence or a clean fragment, no missing verb; count syllables the
  Spanish way (synalepha between words, one more after a stressed last
  syllable, one fewer after an esdrújula) and say the count only when it is
  true; a rhyme is consonant or assonant and is named as what it is; counts
  of stanzas and lines match the text; stress claims follow the Spanish
  stress of the words. Facts about real poems are exact.
- Sounds in a note only where they are the point (lessons 50–55, 64).
  Mastery lessons carry no figured pronunciation, and `figure_es.py --write`
  is not run on them: it figures Foundation levels only (A1–B2) and writes
  nothing at C1 or C2. When a note quotes the Madrid sound of a word, never
  figure it by hand: run `python3 -I work/tools/figure_es.py --say "word"`
  and copy its output with the key's signs exactly (content/es/pronunciation.md,
  English readers); a one-syllable word never carries asterisks. The key
  figures Madrid only: describe another variety's sound in words through
  English, as the key does (the s of mismo breathed like the h of hat; y and
  ll in Buenos Aires like the sh of shop), never with invented signs.
- A note explains only what is in its own sentence, and never claims
  something about another text without rereading it. When a note says who
  does what in the text, check it against the text. A table's columns hold
  what their headers say: check every row.
- Exercise sentences are small scenes, logical on their own, natural and
  self-contained; they restate the lesson's facts exactly (the same person
  does the same thing; a claim about a group holds for every member).
- A revision text introduces nothing the week did not have, reuses the
  week's people with their own names and places, never gives a character
  someone else's act, and repeats what happened exactly.
- Notes never mention the plan, the brief, the writer, copyright or
  originality ("the plan's joke", "original text", "we do not quote ...",
  "invented examples"), and make no remarks about the lesson itself or about
  writing it: the learner never sees the plan. Write "the joke", "the
  ending". Never copy brief instructions into lesson text. No working notes
  to yourself in a lesson file ("(Note: ...)", "check: ...", "fix", "?").
- Model answers in tasks are good, natural Spanish of the asked length and
  register, not a copy of the text; they do what their task asks and meet
  their own rubric (if the rubric asks for usted, the model has usted), and
  add no facts the rubric forbids.
- Swearing (lesson 80) and slang (lesson 81): real, widely known words only,
  each with its register in the note; the strongest words are named in the
  notes, not used by the narrator; no slurs.

Rules carried over from the Dutch Mastery (errors writers really made):

After week 1:
- Formal formulas agree with the addressee: Estimado señor, Estimada señora, Estimados señores (a group or a company), Distinguida señora; the close matches the opening (Atentamente, Un cordial saludo).
- A scene that happens once (a ten-minute meeting with the tutor) cannot refer to "last week's" meeting; references in time must fit the scene.
- When the narrator tells events in order, do not announce with a pluperfect what happens in the next sentence ("Le había ofrecido el tuteo como un regalo. Le dije: ...").
- Similes must say what is meant: "como si pasara por allí por casualidad", not a vague "con una cara como si solo estuviera allí por eso".

After week 2:
- A story or chat over several days keeps its own timeline: nothing can be "coming in two weeks" and "there since yesterday" in the same text.
- The last line is the last line: nothing after "y apagó la luz"; put the punchline last.
- Who offers and who refuses must be consistent in one exchange (the waiter cannot offer what he then says is forbidden).

After week 4:
- Work out clock and time jokes before writing them: a clock that runs five minutes slow strikes twelve five minutes AFTER midnight.
- In a story with corrections (an unreliable narrator), every correction must point the same way: the witnesses do not contradict each other unless that is the joke.

After week 6:
- Never ask the same question twice in one scene, and never let one speaker have two consecutive dialogue lines: merge them into one line.
- Nobody asks a question and answers it in the same line unless "dijo X" marks the change of speaker.
- A sentence starting with "Eso", "Esto" or "Lo" refers to the line just before it: when you add a line (a later reply, a side remark), check that the next line's pronoun still points at the right thing.
- A letter or email quoted in the text contains only what the writer would really send: no postscript telling the reader about the drafts; put that in the narrator's voice.
- Who does what follows from the rules the text states: a handwritten will is written, dated and signed by the testator, so the notary cannot write it for her.
- Revision texts claim only what the earlier texts say: a reply cannot thank someone for a remark her letter never made.
- Facts with dates: "unos años después" must match the real gap.

After week 7:
- An aside to the audience must make sense in the scene: no joke about people reaching for their wallets at a free lecture; an evening lecture ends with a drink, not lunch.
- Names keep their article where Spanish uses one (el Congreso, la Real Academia Española, el Prado); in Spain "doctor" in speech is a medical doctor, so address academics as profesor or profesora, and write Dr. or Dra. only in writing.
- Body positions stay consistent: a candidate who stands at the lectern cannot "sit up straight".
- "Por eso", "así que", "o sea" must follow from the line just before; when they do not, rewrite the link.
- An ex1 or ex2 sentence copies no line whose joke needs the scene; every sentence makes sense alone.

After week 8:
- A character keeps one variety: an Argentine uses vos with everyone he would say tú to (vos tenés, never tú tienes), a Canarian says ustedes, never vosotros; a Madrid character never slips into voseo or into another country's words.
- A character's own definitions stay consistent: if she says "guagua is the bus", she cannot later use guagua for something else unless that is the joke.
- No meta references in a story ("like in the lesson on Mexico"); people in the story do not know they are in a course.
- An answer agrees with the answer before it: do not follow "seseo is southern" with "it is not that simple" unless the text explains why.
- Do not explain a word's or a name's origin unless the facts are certain.

After week 9:
- Before a punchline, the text has set up in one sentence why it is funny; if the essay after a song names the song's last line, quote it exactly.
- A rhyme must be correct Spanish first: change the rhyme word, not the grammar.
- A sketch built on rules ends with a twist that follows from those rules; check every step against the rules as stated.
- A revision quotes the lessons' lines exactly as they now stand, in full sentences, never cut into ungrammatical fragments; no English word in a Spanish line.
- Idioms keep their fixed form and their verb: "no tener ni pies ni cabeza", "no dar pie con bola", "tirar la casa por la ventana"; check every idiom's full form, not only its nouns.
- A satire's title must be what the text is about: say plainly once what the absurd thing is.

After week 10:
- Rhyme lines are full sentences with the right tense and the right verb for their subject.
- A frame story keeps its own facts: a poem written on the way to work is not written "on the way home".
- In a revision, each person says or reads only what is theirs (the caller did not write the presenter's poem); address people by surname with señor or señora (señor Gómez) where the scene is formal.

After week 11:
- A twist ending must be true of the whole text: keep the real topic vague from the first line and reveal it only in the last; do not name it halfway and then "reveal" it again.
- An example of a fallacy must match its own explanation.
- A letter against a plan argues against it with reasons, not by repeating that it is bad.
- People cannot quote what has not happened yet: on Friday a newsroom cannot cite "Saturday's paper" except as its own coming edition.
- Tag every line in a debate with its speaker unless the line before names who answers.

After week 12:
- A revision is ONE scene that happens in time (a lunch, a meeting), where the people talk to each other about what happened in the week; never a string of lines copied from the lessons and given to whoever is at the table. Characters may quote a lesson's line, saying who said it.
- Small talk must still follow: check every reply answers the line before.
- A detail is not asked twice: once the price per kilo is said, nobody asks it again.
- Each first voicemail or message identifies the speaker once ("Soy Gonzalo"), and then does not say "soy yo" in the next sentence.

After week 13:
- Each lesson has its own people: do not reuse a name of the same week for a different person, and do not let a revision family quote a stranger from another lesson ("como dijo el veterinario") or take over another lesson's act.
- Keep tú or usted consistent between two people for the whole scene.
- Fixed formulas complete: "lo menos que podía hacer", "te acompaño en el sentimiento", "mi más sentido pésame".
- An opening line must not contradict the next one ("I'll keep it short ... it would be rude to keep it short").

Rules from the Spanish Foundation that apply here:
- No literal gloss in brackets when it is the same as the translation.
- A note does not say the same thing twice in two sentences.
- refs in a revision lesson name earlier lesson numbers, not note sections.
- Exercise sentences are things a person would say, never a reply with nothing before it.

## Rules added after week 2 (Spanish)

- A chat or group conversation is still a conversation: each message
  answers the one before it, nobody repeats a phrase of the week just to
  use it ("un momentito, y ya está"), and nobody's reply is nonsense
  ("una flauta no tiene pedales").
- The phrases the week teaches go where a speaker would really say them;
  if a phrase does not fit the scene, leave it to the tables.
